# lu-compute-monitoring-jolt
- **URL:** https://jolt.law.harvard.edu/assets/articlePDFs/v39.1/Compute-Monitoring.pdf · **Accessed:** 2026-09-16 (31 pp.; abstract, TOC, Parts III.B.3, III.C, IV read) · **For:** v1 response round, item 11
- **What it establishes:** Lisa Lu (Stanford Law '25), "Compute Monitoring: Reviving the Encryption Backdoor Debate," 39 Harv. J.L. & Tech. 383 (Fall 2025). Thesis: compute-governance regimes that require providers (Amazon, Microsoft, Google) to access encrypted/confidential-computing customer data may be unconstitutional searches, and mandating decryption tools/backdoors is compelled speech (code as expression; Bernstein, Junger; the Apple–San Bernardino dispute). Distinction: reporting "objective compute metrics" (processing power, training duration) is permissible under Zauderer; "subjective assessments or invasive data disclosures" trigger heightened scrutiny. Third-party doctrine: unlikely to extinguish privacy in all cloud data (Riley, Carpenter), but "the Fourth Amendment likely does not protect non-content data, such as compute metrics" (Smith v. Maryland transactional-records line), p. 401. Recommends limiting requests to "objectively defined compute usage metrics or transactional data" and privacy-preserving tech. NOTE: the Clipper chip is never mentioned; her historical parallel is Apple/FBI 2016 (pp. 408–411), not Clipper/key escrow.
- **Key passages:** Abstract (p. 383): "distinguishes between constitutionally permissible reporting of objective compute metrics — such as processing power utilized or training duration — and problematic demands for subjective assessments or invasive data disclosures that implicate protected rights." / p. 401: "the third-party doctrine is not likely to extinguish a reasonable expectation of privacy in all cloud-based data. However, the Fourth Amendment likely does not protect non-content data, such as compute metrics." / p. 408: "routine disclosures of factual, operational data are unlikely to violate the First Amendment under Zauderer. But where reporting requires subjective assessments or burdensome technical changes, courts may apply heightened scrutiny." / p. 410: "Were Amazon, Microsoft, and Google to have to create a backdoor for the government, their assurances to their customers would ring hollow." / p. 411: "a broad encryption backdoor affecting all user accounts would likely not survive a strict scrutiny analysis."
- **Reliability:** Secondary (student Note in a peer-edited law journal); independent.
