# chatrie-v-united-states-slip-opinion
- **URL:** https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/25pdf/25-112_0am4.pdf · **Accessed:** 2026-09-16 (72-page slip opinion read via a reader proxy’s text extraction; syllabus, opinion of the Court, and dissent openings reviewed) · **For:** v1 response round, item 8
- **What it establishes:** Chatrie v. United States, No. 25-112, 609 U.S. ___ (2026), argued Apr. 27, 2026, decided June 29, 2026. Kagan, J., for the Court (Roberts, Sotomayor, Kavanaugh, Jackson); Jackson concurring (with Sotomayor); Gorsuch concurring in judgment; Alito dissenting (Thomas as to Part I; Barrett in part); Barrett dissenting. Held: acquiring Chatrie's Location History from Google was a Fourth Amendment search; reasonableness of the multi-step geofence warrant remanded. The Court applies Carpenter's two features—the data is "incomparably 'revealing'" and "not truly 'shared'" because smartphone services are "indispensable"—and rejects the government's opt-in (voluntariness) and two-hour-duration arguments. It expressly declines to extend the third-party doctrine to Location History.
- **Key passages:** Syllabus: "Held: Police officers conducted a Fourth Amendment search when they acquired Chatrie's location data from Google because an individual has a reasonable expectation of privacy in his cell-phone location information. Pp. 10–33." / Op. at 2: police "intrude on that constitutionally protected interest when they demand the information—even though for only a limited time, and from a third-party tech company." / Op. at 29: "we hold that police officers invade a cell-phone user's reasonable expectation of privacy when they access his Location History. ... Nor does it matter that the materials obtained were handed over by a third-party tech company." / Op. at 29: "A cell-phone user is not to be viewed as sharing private information with third parties—which then can be freely passed on to the government—just by doing the ordinary things cell-phone users do." / Op. at 28: "that is to imagine that all of us are living in dumb flip-phone days. The point of carrying smartphones is to use what is on them".
- **Reliability:** Primary (slip opinion). Page numbers are slip-opinion pages of the Opinion of the Court.
- **Relevance to this project:** Item 8; the most recent Supreme Court statement narrowing the third-party doctrine, decided after CRS LSB11429; supports the argument that a chatbot record, if "revealing" and generated by "indispensable" services, could fall inside Carpenter—though the Court did not address chatbot data.
