# bublitz-2026-what-is-thought

Blitz (eds.), *The Law and Ethics of Freedom of Thought, Volume 2* (Palgrave Macmillan, Cham, 2026), ch. 8, pp. 217–254, doi 10.1007/978-3-031-91466-9_8. **Open Access, CC BY 4.0** ("Open Access funded by the project PRIVETDIS"). - **What it establishes:** All three review-quoted sentences are verbatim. BUT the chapter **never mentions the extended mind, Blitz 2010, diaries, devices, search histories or chat logs** (0 hits for "extended mind"; McCarthy-Jones appears once, in a footnote, as "a view from a psychiatrist"). It is an interpretation of "thought" aimed at neuroimaging. Its bearing on the essay is therefore *structural and inferential*, not a direct published objection to externalized records: (i) thought = mental events; (ii) external manifestations of thought belong largely to Art. 19; (iii) the privacy test is confined to "physiological correlates of unexpressed thought"; (iv) absolute rights must be construed restrictively, with a qualified right (privacy) catching the rest — the "two-layer model"; (v) treating what a thought is *about* (or, by extension, its trace) as "thought" is "crossing the line from the internum to the externum and leading ad absurdum." The "generalizations from the externum to the internum" sentence is, in context, an argument *against Ligthart's narrowing* of the internum by analogy from forum-externum case law — i.e., it is deployed to make the internum BROADER, not to exclude external records; but its "vice versa" does cut against any argument that transfers the internum's features to external acts.
- **Key passages (page = printed page):**
  - p. 223 (Robust-scope view, the one he adopts): "A broader 'Robust-scope view' conceives of 'thought' as encompassing two mental events commonly understood as such: thoughts as a type of occurrent mental state with content, and thinking as a mental action. Accordingly, the right comprises a range of mental activities such as calculating, imagining, or interpreting legal norms as well as decision-making, and the resulting mental states such as memories."
  - pp. 219–220: "Article 18(1) ICCPR does not directly address external manifestations of thought, unlike manifestations of religious or conscientious beliefs. Many ways to manifest thought fall under the ambit of the subsequent Article 19 ICCPR, freedom of expression (sometimes mistakenly understood synonymously with freedom of thought)."
  - p. 233 (§ "The Crucial Difference Between Externum and Internum"): "But the reasons for narrowly understanding belief regarding the externum do not apply to the internum, as unmanifested mental events lack the potential to adversely affect others in direct, legally relevant ways. This allows for a broader understanding of the internum. Due to this structural difference, generalizations from the externum to the internum, and vice versa, are not readily possible."
  - p. 234: "This reasoning also partly explains the unconditional protection of freedom of thought: because the law's legitimacy derives from regulating external conduct, coercing thought is ultra vires." … "Structural, historical, and normative differences between the internum and the externum forbid generalizations."
  - p. 236 (on *Salonen v. Finland*, child's name as "thought"): "the scope of the right would comprise features of what thought is about. This conflates 'thought' with the contents or objects of thought, crossing the line from the internum to the externum and leading ad absurdum."
  - p. 239 (§ "Restrictive Construction of Absolute Rights"): "The most important constructive argument arises from the unconditional nature of freedom of thought. Practical legal wisdom demands construing such rights restrictively. Without room for balancing, the rigidity and inflexibility of unconditional rights often complicate nuanced decisions." … "Such is the tragedy of absolute rights: while their supreme value commands utmost respect, they are narrowly defined or overlooked. Transforming freedom of thought into a living, practically effective right may require, nolens volens, a narrow construction."
  - p. 239 (§ "Two-Layered Protection of the Mind"): "it must be ensured cases falling outside of the narrowly constructed scope of the right are addressed by a broader qualified right. This suggests a two-layer model: a broader qualified right alongside a narrower unconditional right, which jointly provide comprehensive protection yet allow nuanced decision." … "Prime candidates are the rights to bodily or mental integrity addressing the former, and the right to privacy addressing the latter."
  - p. 244: "The key condition prompting freedom of thought protection is the revelation of unexpressed thought—those which rightholders have not manifested through voluntary actions such as speech."
  - p. 246 (his test): "Technological measurements of physiological correlates of unexpressed thought, or physiological traces thereof, that allow inferences about the content of thought or the type of thinking a person performs, interfere with freedom of thought, provided they attain a level of seriousness and are conducted without consent."
  - p. 246: "Correlations of thought not publicly visible such as brain waves may merit stronger protection than publicly visible ones such as facial expressions. Given the partly public nature of faces, there must be room for balancing, which speaks against full freedom of thought protection."
  - p. 248 (conclusion): "the picture of a two-layer model emerges: freedom of thought addresses grave interferences with thought and thinking, while qualified rights to privacy and integrity address less intensive interferences."
  - **Concessions useful to the essay:**
    - p. 243: "However, problems begin once these natural capacities are significantly augmented by (digital) technologies that afford reliable, otherwise unattainable insights into thought, especially its content."
    - p. 245: "This view is supported by statements applying freedom of thought to digital technologies. The Office of the High Commissioner for Human Rights argues that Spyware that secretly records user interaction with digital devices may intrude deeply into people's lifes and allow inferences about unrevealed inner aspects that it violates freedom of thought. Moreover, the Committee on the Rights of the Child considers automated assessments of a child's inner state to run counter to freedom thought. These are clear statements arguing that interferences with freedom of thought do not need to involve compulsion in a narrow sense." [sic throughout]
    - p. 245 (rationale = the essay's rationale): "The deeper reason for considering non-forceful means as potential interferences with freedom of thought lies in the rationale of the right. The primary concern is not so much the use of force, often unlawful per se, but the revelation of the unexpressed, the intrusion into the mind, into a sphere of seclusion. Privacy of thought is a precondition for free thinking. The fear that unexpressed thought might be registered and revealed, made public or somehow used against the thinker, would lead people to self-censor their thinking—the epitome of unfree though."
    - pp. 245–246: "If protection were afforded to specific thoughts only, thinkers would inevitably feel compelled to avoid certain lines of thinking to remain free from surveillance. … Free thinking therefore requires privacy of all thoughts."
    - p. 249: "By drawing a bright line of normative inaccessibility around a sphere which was largely taken to be inaccessible for millennia, the present proposal merely affirms long-held presuppositions." (He too states the factual-inaccessibility premise: "The factual invincibility of thought … was largely assumed for centuries.")
    - p. 233–234: "At the moment, there is no established case law that guides or prefigures the understanding of 'thought'. Everything is up for interpretation."

- **Relevance to this project:** §III's "descends from … the right's rationale, not its unlimited scope." Bublitz supplies (a) the strongest structural objection — absolute scope must be narrow, external manifestations go to Art. 19/privacy, and internum↔externum generalizations "are not readily possible"; and (b) the essay's own rationale in his words (p. 245) plus an opening: he accepts that OHCHR treats spyware-on-devices inference as a freedom-of-thought violation. The essay's "what descends" answer can be framed inside his two-layer model: the rationale (p. 245) grounds a *qualified but heightened* layer for records — which is exactly what he says must exist outside the absolute core. Do NOT describe this chapter as objecting to the extended-mind move by name; the by-name objection is Bublitz 2013 fn 23 (see bublitz-2013-my-mind-is-mine) and, per De Cock, Ligthart/Bublitz et al. 2022 HRLR (not opened this round).
