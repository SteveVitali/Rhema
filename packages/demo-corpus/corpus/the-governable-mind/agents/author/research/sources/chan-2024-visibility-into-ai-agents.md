# chan-2024-visibility-into-ai-agents
- **URL:** https://arxiv.org/html/2401.13138v4 (v4, 10 Apr 2024; full HTML read locally) · metadata cross-checked at https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.13138 and Crossref · **Accessed:** 2026-09-16 · **For:** v2→v3 response round, slice N (item N1)
- **What it establishes:** The canonical governance paper on agent logging defines the logged object to INCLUDE user inputs and makes user attribution a stated purpose. It does not separate "a log of what a system did at an interface" from "a log of what a person asked it"; both sit inside one "activity log." The same paper names the surveillance risk and proposes graded access ("minimum necessary", de-identified vs identifiable, third-party adjudicator).
- **Key passages:**
  - §3.3 Activity Logs: "Activity logs are records of certain inputs and outputs of an agent. Inputs and outputs could be from both users or tools and services, as we illustrate in Figure 1." Next: "Logs can include varying levels of detail and may only record actions above a certain degree of significance, depending on the behaviour one is trying to track, privacy considerations, and storage requirements."
  - §3.3: "Activity logs can inform an understanding of the agent's impacts as well as allow one to tie behaviour to specific user choices, facilitating post-incident attribution and forensics (O'Brien et al., 2023)."
  - §3.1 (final sentence of the section intro, immediately before the §3.1.1 heading — NOT inside §3.1.1): "Unique identifiers for each AI agent could facilitate accountability by linking an action to an AI agent and its user, developer(s), and deployer."
  - §3.3.1: "Different categories of logs could exist, such as for tool use, internal reasoning (Wei et al., 2023), self-critique (Saunders et al., 2022), memory management, etc"
  - §3.3.2: "More detailed logging is more useful, but may impose more significant costs on the deployer, require more resources and expertise for analysis, and pose more significant privacy concerns."
  - §3.4 Risks: "In general, if agents substitute for humans in a wide variety of activities, information about those agents might be tantamount to information about the users of those agents. Indeed, agent activities may be easier to monitor than human activities because deployers are a central intermediary. Governments or deployers may thus abuse their power to carry out excessive or unjustified surveillance of personal activities (Goodwin, 2018; Greenwald and MacAskill, 2013). These considerations justify limiting data collection in accordance with the risk of the agent's activities or domain of deployment."
  - §3.4: "Records and logs can be de-identified with respect to individual users or identifiable." and "The granularity and quantity of access should be the minimum necessary for the accessing party to achieve its (legitimate) objectives. When investigations pertain to specific users, identifiable information could be made available upon request given a showing of compelling need and/or after approval from a third-party adjudicator."
  - §3.4 also records existing practice the other way: "Language model APIs with no logging of inputs or outputs".
  - §4.3 Risks: "Given that a handful of compute providers dominates the market (Richter, 2023), monitoring users of those providers would equivalent to monitoring much of society." (sic — the source omits "be"; quote with "[be]").
- **Reliability:** Primary; peer-reviewed (FAccT 2024). Authors are GovAI/Mila/Harvard/Oxford/Cambridge/Toronto governance researchers, i.e. proponents of visibility measures who nonetheless state the surveillance objection themselves.
- **Relevance to this project:** Bears on the essay's action-log/prompt-log distinction. The review's point holds: the literature's definition bundles user inputs and user attribution into the log. But the paper also supplies the essay's counter-move (de-identified logs, minimum-necessary access, identifiable data only on showing of need), so the distinction the essay wants is available as a design choice the paper itself contemplates, not as the literature's default definition. Citation: Alan Chan, Carson Ezell, Max Kaufmann, Kevin Wei, Lewis Hammond, Herbie Bradley, Emma Bluemke, Nitarshan Rajkumar, David Krueger, Noam Kolt, Lennart Heim, Markus Anderljung, "Visibility into AI Agents," in The 2024 ACM Conference on Fairness, Accountability, and Transparency (FAccT '24), Rio de Janeiro, June 3–6, 2024, pp. 958–973, doi:10.1145/3630106.3658948 (pages/DOI per Crossref); arXiv:2401.13138.
