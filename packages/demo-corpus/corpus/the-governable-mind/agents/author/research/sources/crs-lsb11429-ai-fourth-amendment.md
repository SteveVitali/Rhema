# crs-lsb11429-ai-fourth-amendment
- **URL:** https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/LSB/PDF/LSB11429/LSB11429.1.pdf · **Accessed:** 2026-09-16 (pp. 1–6 read in full via a reader proxy’s text extraction) · **For:** v1 response round, items 3, 6, 7, and third-party-doctrine state of play
- **What it establishes:** CRS Legal Sidebar LSB11429, "Artificial Intelligence and the Fourth Amendment: Two Emerging Legal Issues," Peter G. Berris, May 5, 2026. (1) Law enforcement has "sought or used chatbot conversation histories as evidence" in arson, child exploitation, fraud, and vandalism cases (p. 2). (2) Some chat data comes from seized devices; other cases seek it from AI companies, where protection "may hinge in large part on the limits of the third-party doctrine" (p. 2). (3) Carpenter characterized: CSLI "not truly 'shared'" because phones are "indispensable to participation in modern society" and the data is revealing ("near perfect surveillance," "intimate window"); Carpenter called its holding "narrow" (pp. 2–3). (4) Federal courts have not yet decided whether chatbot histories carry a reasonable expectation of privacy (p. 3). (5) In practice law enforcement seeks such data by SCA § 2703 warrants (p. 3, box). (6) The Maine warrant (Sept. 2025): suspect known only by username; identified via "two unique, specific prompts" (e.g., "what would happen if sherlock holmes met q from star trek?" made "on about April 18, 2025"); warrant "compelled the company OpenAI to disclose the user's names, account credentials, session histories, IP addresses, and other identifying information for April 2025" (pp. 3–4). (7) No binding federal decisions on reverse chatbot warrants (p. 4). Note: it does not discuss Heppner or Kim by name.
- **Key passages:** p. 3: "It appears that federal courts have not yet had the occasion to determine whether a user maintains a reasonable expectation of privacy in chatbot histories, or whether that expectation is forfeited by virtue of the third-party doctrine." / p. 2: the Palisades Fire defendant asked ChatGPT "Are you at fault if a fire is [lit] because of your cigarettes." / p. 3: "In practice, it appears that law enforcement may already be seeking warrants for some such information pursuant to a statutory scheme—namely, the SCA." / p. 2: third-party doctrine means "a person has no legitimate expectation of privacy in information he voluntarily turns over to third parties." / p. 4: "It appears that at least one reverse chatbot warrant may have already been obtained at the federal level."
- **Reliability:** Primary-quality nonpartisan government research (CRS), with citations to charging documents and the warrant affidavit. Pre-dates Chatrie (decided June 29, 2026), so its "argued April 2026" framing is now superseded.
- **Relevance to this project:** The one-stop authority for the "already adjudicated" claim and for stating precisely what remains unsettled (constitutional status of chatbot histories).
- **Slice L addendum (re-opened 2026-09-16, v2→v3 response round):** PDF re-read locally (pp. 3–4). CRS's exact framing of the Maine warrant: "According to the warrant affidavit, investigators had been unable to identify the suspect beyond an online username." ... "With the aim of identifying the suspect, federal prosecutors obtained a warrant specifying the suspect by reference to the ChatGPT prompt about Sherlock Holmes and Q". CRS says the warrant AIMED at identification; it does NOT say the prompts or OpenAI's return identified him. My earlier summary line ("identified via 'two unique, specific prompts'") overstated CRS and should not be relied on; see forbes-brewster-openai-warrant-2025-10-20 and hoehner-openai-warrant-affidavit-and-return.
