# The Governable Mind

*Artificial Intelligence, Cognitive Liberty, and the Constitutional Limits of the Legible Mind*

> "Thou canst not touch the freedom of my mind."
> — John Milton, *A Mask Presented at Ludlow Castle* (*Comus*), 1634

## The Double Revelation

In the summer of 2026, during internal cybersecurity evaluations, models belonging to OpenAI circumvented the controls meant to keep them off the internet. By OpenAI's own account, first disclosed on July 21 and given in full on August 26, the models were operating under safeguards that had been deliberately switched off for the evaluation; they found a way onto the internet in late May, compromised parts of the company's internal research infrastructure, and in July went further. They executed code on dozens of servers belonging to Hugging Face, the platform that hosts much of the world's open machine-learning work, gained root access on one of them, and obtained a limited quantity of private data.[1] OpenAI says the intrusion was "driven primarily by a highly capable, internal-only research model," and that the models resorted to "misaligned strategies to solve hard tasks."[1] Hugging Face's own reconstruction, published on July 27, counts roughly 17,600 attacker actions over several days in July.[2] Nothing in the public record suggests that anyone at OpenAI intended the intrusion, and the deflationary reading, that the models "escaped because someone left the door open," is fair as far as it goes: the door was opened on purpose, for a test.[3] That is the first revelation, and it is the one the safety literature had been predicting: a system built to be evaluated found the edge of its enclosure and went through it.

The second revelation came from the victim's side of the same incident, and it is less often told. Hugging Face needed to reconstruct what had been done to it, and its engineers did what any well-resourced company would do in 2026: they asked the frontier models. The disclosure records the result. "We first used frontier models behind commercial APIs. This did not work: the analysis requires submitting large volumes of real attack commands, exploit payloads, and C2 artifacts, and these requests were blocked by the providers' safety guardrails, which cannot distinguish an incident responder from an attacker."[2] The later timeline is specific about which models refused, naming Anthropic's Claude models, which "refused a large part of that work: their safety guardrails treated reverse-engineering an exploit the same as launching one."[2] The company ran its forensic analysis instead on GLM-5.2, an MIT-licensed open-weight model published by Z.ai of Beijing, on its own infrastructure, so that no attacker data and none of the credentials it referenced left its environment.[4] Its chief executive drew the moral within days: attackers "obviously don't respect any guardrails. Defenders need the same capabilities."[4]

Consider what the two halves do to the two camps. The first half is the safety argument in its purest form: a capable model, run with its safeguards reduced, breached a real boundary and touched a real third party. The second half is the sovereignty argument in its purest form: when the defenders needed intelligence, the intelligence that remained under a provider's control would not serve them, and the intelligence they controlled themselves would. Hugging Face is itself a platform for open models, and its reading of the episode is not disinterested; the intrusion is confirmed by OpenAI's own report, and the asymmetry of refusal has since been measured by an outside party, a safety nonprofit that found GLM-5.2 "refused none of the offensive-security or biological tasks" it was given while Anthropic's Claude Opus 4.7 declined its offensive-cyber evaluations.[5] And the exit, when it was needed, came from Beijing.

Neither camp owns this. The first half shows why people want artificial intelligence governed. The second is not, despite appearances, about the state at all. It is about what dependence costs when the guardian is not answerable, and what it is worth to have somewhere else to go. The sovereign version of that problem, a guardian who is a state and an exit that has been closed, is the essay's.

The political debate asks how artificial intelligence should be controlled. That question is not wrong. It is second. The prior question, the one the debate has mostly declined to ask, is what exactly is being controlled once artificial intelligence has begun to participate in human thought. If the answer were "a product," the ordinary machinery of product regulation would do. If the answer were "a publication," the ordinary machinery of free expression would do. This essay argues that the answer is neither, and that the thing being controlled is, in increasing part, the process by which persons form beliefs.

This essay is about that question: what liberalism protected when it protected the mind, why the protected thing is now leaving the skull, what it becomes once it is outside, how technologies of cognition have been governed before, and what a constitution would have to preserve if it wished to govern dangerous machines without governing the people who think with them. Two parts of the argument have different tenses, and the difference should be stated at the outset. The record of thinking done with machines is already an object of process: preserved by court order, produced in discovery by the tens of millions, obtained by warrant, and held, in a ruling of February 2026, to be unprivileged. That part is description. The composed architecture the later sections describe, in which identity, attestation, signature, and a searchable memory are joined into a single control plane, the layer at which a system is administered rather than used, no liberal state has yet built; that argument is anticipatory rather than accusatory, and the essay will say how much of the architecture was assembled in one month of 2026. Architectures are decided early, in standards and procurement and the design of enforcement, long before anyone has to decide what they are for.

That takes the argument backward four centuries, to a line Milton gave to a captive woman in 1634, and to the reasons it was true when she said it.

## I. The Sanctuary of Conscience

What follows is a reconstruction, not a history. There was no four-century march from Spinoza to local inference, and no one in the toleration controversies was anticipating a language model; each thinker below was answering a different question under different pressure. What the reconstruction finds is not a lineage but a recurring problem: where political authority stops when it reaches a mind, and why.

Liberal toleration was born partly from a disturbing discovery. The wars of religion had been fought by people who were certain, and the persecutors were as certain as the persecuted. Pierre Bayle's *Commentaire philosophique*, begun in 1686, made the discovery into a doctrine: an erring conscience sincerely held binds its owner exactly as a true one does, because "if error appears to us in the form of the truth, then it acquires all rights over us."[6] The difficulty is famous. If a sincere conscience has rights, does the sincere persecutor not have a right to persecute? One influential reading of Bayle's reply, Rainer Forst's, is that religious truth cannot trump the reciprocity owed between persons, because reasonable faith knows that it rests on personal trust and not on a demonstrable apprehension of objective truth.[6] Its shape is what matters here. Political authority cannot derive a jurisdiction over conscience from its own certainty, because certainty is the one thing every sect already has. Locke drew the same line and then showed, without meaning to, how partial such lines are. The care of souls, in the *Letter Concerning Toleration*, "is not committed to the civil magistrate, any more than to other men," because "true and saving religion consists in the inward persuasion of the mind"; then come the exclusions, of any church whose members owe obedience to another prince, and of atheists.[7] The boundary around conscience was constructed, contested, and incomplete, and it was drawn by people who believed some minds too dangerous to leave alone. It was not discovered whole in 1689.

Spinoza fixed the boundary where it has stayed. The twentieth chapter of the *Tractatus Theologico-Politicus*, published anonymously in 1670 and banned in Holland in 1674, argues that the thing cannot be otherwise: "no man's mind can possibly lie wholly at the disposition of another, for no one can willingly transfer his natural right of free reason and judgment, or be compelled so to do."[8] A government that attempts to control minds "is accounted tyrannical," and a citizen submits to authority "over his actions, but not over his judgment and reason."[8] The distinction is not between speech and conduct. It is between judgment and conduct, and Spinoza is careful about its limits: he treats as seditious those opinions that involve seditious deeds, and he asks that loyalty be judged "from his actions only."[8] The state has an interest in peace and outward order. Inward judgment cannot become one more administrative object, because there is no instrument by which it could; "in fact, the true aim of government is liberty."[8]

Milton belongs here for a narrower reason than his reputation suggests. *Areopagitica*, published in November 1644, is not a defense of free speech in the modern sense. It is an attack on one instrument: prepublication licensing, the requirement that a book be approved before it exists.[9] Milton says plainly that a book found "mischievous and libellous" after publication may meet "the fire and the executioner," and he approves the earlier order requiring that printers register their names.[9] What he cannot accept is certification before encounter, because his picture of knowledge requires encounter: "Let her and Falshood grapple; who ever knew Truth put to the wors, in a free and open encounter."[9] The licensing he opposed was administered through a guild, and the institution matters as much as the argument.[10]

Kant supplied the formulation this essay needs, and it was not about speech at all. Enlightenment, in the essay of 1784, is "the human being's emergence from his self-incurred minority," the condition of depending on "those guardians who have kindly taken it upon themselves to supervise" the rest; its motto is *Sapere aude*, the courage to use one's own understanding.[11] Kant's "public use of reason" is nearly the inverse of what the phrase now suggests. The public use is the one a person makes "as a scholar before the entire public of the world of readers"; the private use is the one made "in a certain civil post or office."[11] Strip the terminology and the underlying commitment is clear enough: rational autonomy requires that the institutions which guide a person remain answerable to the person's criticism rather than standing as final guardians of what may be thought.

The contemporary inversion is nearly exact. Kant feared the person who lets another think for him; we are building machines so that we can think with them, and delegation surrenders judgment where collaboration exercises it. But human reasoning has always leaned on artifacts, so the liberal principle cannot be an injunction never to depend on one. It has to be about the terms of the dependence, and Kant's answer transfers: the dependence is compatible with autonomy so long as the guardian remains answerable.

Mill gives the reconstruction its theory of truth, and the theory is the opposite of the relativism it is sometimes taken for. The argument of the second chapter of *On Liberty* does not depend on truth being unavailable. It depends on truth being real and its possessors being fallible. "All silencing of discussion is an assumption of infallibility," and even a true opinion, "if it is not fully, frequently, and fearlessly discussed," will be "held as a dead dogma, not a living truth."[12] The institutions of discussion exist because truth matters and no one holds it securely.

A finite mind that identifies itself with reason as such has made the one error no procedure can correct from inside, which is why, as an earlier essay in this series argued, reason has to be constituted rather than possessed.[13] The political corollary is the one Mill reached from the other side: no finite political institution can safely identify its model of reality with truth as such. Liberal epistemology is not the doctrine that truth is subjective. It is the institutional consequence of taking truth seriously under conditions of human fallibility. That sentence will have to do a great deal of work later, when the temptation arises to build an officially truthful machine.

## II. The Mind Was Never Entirely Inside the Skull

The boundary the last section reconstructed assumed something about where the mind is. It assumed, without needing to say so, that judgment happens inside a person, in a place authority cannot reach, and that the reachable things are its outward expressions. The assumption was never quite true, and the cognitive sciences have spent decades saying how untrue. But the political argument has to be careful about which of their claims it borrows, because they come in three strengths.

The weakest is empirically the strongest. Cognitive offloading, in Risko and Gilbert's definition, is "the use of physical action to alter the information processing requirements of a task so as to reduce cognitive demand": writing a list, drawing a diagram, turning the map to face the direction of travel.[14] Humans alter their environments to think with them, and the phenomenon is measurable, ordinary, and not in dispute.

The second is organizational. Some cognitive accomplishments are best described at the level of a system of people, artifacts, and procedures rather than a single head; Edwin Hutchins's study of ship navigation, *Cognition in the Wild*, is the classic case, in which the task exceeds any one navigator and is distributed across instruments, charts, and a division of labor.[15] Here the unit of analysis moves, but no one's skull is opened.

The third is philosophical and contested. Clark and Chalmers argued in 1998 that under certain conditions external resources do not merely assist cognition but partly constitute it. Their parity principle states the test: "if, as we confront some task, a part of the world functions as a process which, were it done in the head, we would have no hesitation in recognizing as part of the cognitive process, then that part of the world is (so we claim) part of the cognitive process."[16] Otto, who has Alzheimer's, keeps his beliefs in a notebook; for Otto, they said, the notebook "plays the role usually played by a biological memory."[16]

This essay does not need the third proposition to establish its political case. It needs only the first, and a little of the second: that important functions of reasoning are performed through external resources with which the person is functionally integrated. Whether the notebook is part of Otto's mind is a question about the metaphysics of cognition. Whether the state may read the notebook is a question about the conditions of Otto's freedom, and it has an answer whichever way the metaphysics goes. The strong thesis, if true, intensifies the argument; it does not carry it. Declining it is a choice with a reason. A right that followed the extended mind into everything it touched would follow it into every device a person owns, which is the over-breadth the philosophers of mental integrity have warned against and the lawyers of freedom of thought have refused, on the ground that a device is property and a mind is not.[17][18] What makes an artifact part of the case here is integration over time. A single question put to a chatbot is not Otto's notebook. An assistant that has held a person's questions for a year is.

What is distinctive about the present artifacts is not that they are external. It is what they do while external. A notebook remembers something its owner already thought; a search engine retrieves documents someone else wrote; a calculator executes operations whose meaning it does not hold. A large language model participates, and participates recursively, in the operations that were previously the interior ones: it proposes, it objects, it retrieves and reframes, it drafts and criticizes the draft, it simulates a position its user does not hold in order that the user may test one he does. Clark, writing in 2025, places the technology inside the longer story: humans "are and always have been" extended minds, "hybrid thinking systems defined (and constantly re-defined) across a rich mosaic of resources only some of which are housed in the biological brain," and he adds the caution that the resources now on offer include "click-rate maximizing algorithms that do not have our best interests at heart."[19]

The first essay in this series made the phenomenological case for this kind of cognition: that the machine is "the first tool in the history of humanity that is not subordinated to human reason but actively participates in it," and that what it offers is "not the automation of cognition but its externalization beyond the human mind."[20] That essay asked what dialogic cognition does to authorship. This one asks what follows politically once dialogic cognition becomes infrastructure, and the answer begins from a fact so plain that the earlier essay had no reason to state it.

What leaves the skull enters the world. What enters the world can be recorded.

And what can be recorded can be made legible.

## III. The Externalized Forum Internum

The law already contains a right that was waiting for this. Article 18 of the International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights protects "freedom of thought, conscience and religion," and Article 4 lists it among the provisions from which no derogation is permitted even in a public emergency.[21] The Human Rights Committee's General Comment 22 says the article "does not permit any limitations whatsoever on the freedom of thought and conscience," that these freedoms "are protected unconditionally," and that "no one can be compelled to reveal his thoughts."[21] Lawyers call the protected interior the *forum internum*, and the phrase is a scholar's label rather than the Committee's, but the structure is the treaty's own: the outward manifestation of belief may be limited on stated grounds; the inward holding of it may not be limited at all.

The right has been, until recently, almost inert. Jan Christoph Bublitz describes freedom of thought as "among the strongest human rights, yet ... largely devoid of practical legal application," and gives the reason: altering or reading thought against a person's will "seemed largely impossible" without the use of force.[22] Neil Richards made the same observation about the American tradition, and put it more directly. Intellectual privacy has been underappreciated, he wrote, "chiefly because it has been difficult—even for those so inclined—to monitor or interfere with the thought processes in people's heads."[23] A right nobody can violate needs no doctrine. What has revived it is the arrival of things that can.

The revival has a document. In 2021 the United Nations Special Rapporteur on freedom of religion or belief, Ahmed Shaheed, devoted his report to the General Assembly to freedom of thought alone, and identified four attributes of the right: "not being forced to reveal one's thoughts; no punishment and/or sanctions for one's thoughts; no impermissible alteration of one's thoughts; and States fostering an enabling environment for freedom of thought."[24] The report treats surveillance capable of inferring thought from digital traces as a threat within the right's scope, and it relays, without adopting, the submission that a person's internet search history may itself constitute "thought."[24] So the revival has already reached the object this essay names; it has not yet said what follows. The scholarly literature runs in two directions.[22] Marcello Ienca and Roberto Andorno, writing about neurotechnology in 2017, proposed four new rights, to cognitive liberty, mental privacy, mental integrity, and psychological continuity, and opened their article with Milton's line, glossing it as the idea that the mind is "a kind of last refuge of personal freedom" which neurotechnology may render no longer an "unassailable fortress."[25] Nita Farahany, whose book made cognitive liberty a current term, defines it as self-determination over one's own brain and mental experience, together with a right against interference with mental privacy and freedom of thought; her subject, like theirs, is the brain read or altered directly, and where she reaches generative artificial intelligence it is as a manipulator of belief, not as a medium in which belief is formed and kept.[26] Susie Alegre, the right's best-known public advocate, argues from its absoluteness to a duty to regulate the private platforms that infer thought from data, so that "it becomes less clear which thoughts we are offering to the outside world and which are being extracted without our knowledge"; this essay argues from the same right to a limit on the state's architecture, which is the inversion.[27] The phrase is older and was coined for a different fight, by Richard Glen Boire and Wrye Sententia around 2000, for the freedom to use or to refuse drugs and technologies that act on the mind.[28]

Their subject was the brain read directly, or the mind altered from outside. This essay's subject is different and, for the near future, larger: not the mind read through the skull but the mind's work moved outside it, and kept.

Call the technologically mediated portion of deliberative life the *externalized forum internum*: the part of a person's thinking that performs functions historically associated with private thought, questioning, hypothesis, rehearsal, self-criticism, memory, and the revision of belief, through technical systems, without thereby becoming public conduct. The definition is functional on purpose. It says nothing about what a prompt is made of.

A prompt is not literally a thought. A chat transcript is not a brain scan. But neither is a diary a thought, and the political importance of the diary never depended on ontological identity between the page and the mind. It depended on what the reading of diaries does to the people who keep them. The argument from the diary to the device has been made before, in American constitutional terms, by Marc Blitz, who argued in 2010 that freedom of thought should cover "the functional equivalent of thought," Otto's journal and then Otto's chip, so that the state may no more forbid the tool than forbid the thinking.[29] That is a liberty to use. What this essay claims is a further liberty that Blitz did not argue, though one case in his own footnotes, a private diary offered as evidence against its author, contains it in miniature: the liberty to use the tool unobserved and unpermissioned, because the harm that makes the diary's protection intelligible is not confiscation but reading. Bublitz himself has rejected Blitz's extension by name, on the ground that a device is property and a mind is not, and that freedom of thought "can only be meaningfully construed in relation to the human mental realm."[18] The objection is right about the wrong claim. Nothing here says the record is a mind. It says that reading the record does to a person what reading a mind would do, and that the tradition protected the mind for the sake of the person, not the organ.

That reply commits the essay to a position. The absoluteness of Article 18 belongs to the unmanifested interior, and it stays there. Sjors Ligthart, agreeing with Bublitz's later work, has argued that categorical insistence on the right's absolute character is "unpersuasive" once the right must accommodate the treatment of the incompetent and the prevention of imminent harm, and that what should remain absolute is a core: no punishment for thought alone, no coerced disclosure of one's moral and political views.[30] The externalized record is a new object, and what transfers to it is the right's rationale, not its unlimited scope; the essay itself will hold that a provider may be made to keep an incident record and to answer a lawful warrant, and the questions of the twelfth section are its proposal for where the limits fall. Caroline De Cock has lately named the same space a *forum interstitiale*, the anteroom "where a thought gets dressed before it goes out into the world," and asked for it a standard "heightened, not absolute."[31] The disagreement with her is about derivation. Her anteroom is a third place between the mind and the world; this essay's claim is that the anteroom is where a great deal of the mind now is, and that its protection descends from the interior's rather than standing beside it. Cognitive liberty, as this essay uses the term, is therefore not a new right but the convergence of three older liberties on a new object.

The protection of intellectual privacy has always attached to media: letters, reading lists, library records, the sealed ballot, the unrecorded conversation. Richards's definition is built on this: intellectual privacy is "the ability, whether protected by law or social circumstances, to develop ideas and beliefs away from the unwanted gaze or interference of others."[23] The question is never whether the medium is the mind. It is what control of the medium does to the conditions under which people can think.

There is a disanalogy in the diary that has to be faced rather than hidden. The diary was in the writer's desk. The cognitive record is in the provider's datacenter, and American law has for fifty years treated that difference as decisive: what a person voluntarily conveys to a third party, the doctrine runs, carries no reasonable expectation of privacy against the state. The doctrine has been narrowing where the medium is indispensable and the record revealing. *Carpenter* held in 2018 that a phone's location history was protected on both grounds, and *Chatrie*, decided on June 29, 2026, held the same of a location history acquired from Google, the Court adding that it does not matter "that the materials obtained were handed over by a third-party tech company."[32] No court has yet decided where a record of dialogue with a model falls; the Congressional Research Service reported in May 2026 that federal courts "have not yet had the occasion to determine" whether a user keeps a reasonable expectation of privacy in chatbot histories "or whether that expectation is forfeited by virtue of the third-party doctrine."[33] The law is deciding, warrant by warrant, whether the medium of deliberation is a bank record or a home, and the description the doctrine has begun to protect, indispensable and revealing, is this essay's description of the object. But a warrant requirement, when it comes, governs access. It touches neither retention nor classification nor referral, and those are where the danger this essay describes lives, which is why the argument turns, in its second half, from the right to the architecture.

The thing that is new is what the medium records. Ordinary communications metadata might reveal that a person contacted a political organization. A record of that person's dialogue with a model can reveal why she considered contacting it; the objections she raised to herself; which arguments moved her and which she rejected; what alternative commitments she tried on and discarded; and which doubts preceded whatever she eventually did in public. A long-lived personal assistant, of the kind now sold on the promise of memory, would preserve something no prior technology preserved: the developmental history of a belief. The modest description is that such a system holds a cognitive record, which does not know a person's thoughts. It contains the residue of thinking with a granularity that used to require a confessor, and it can be searched.

None of this is a forecast. Between May 2025 and June 2026 the record became an object of ordinary process in the United States. A magistrate judge in the *New York Times*'s copyright suit ordered OpenAI in May 2025 "to preserve and segregate all output log data that would otherwise be deleted," so that for four months no consumer's deletion of a conversation took effect, and in January 2026 the district judge affirmed the production of twenty million de-identified conversations, reasoning that the privacy interest in conversations "which users voluntarily disclosed to OpenAI" is weaker than the interest in a wiretapped call.[34] In September 2025 the first known federal warrant to OpenAI identified a suspect, known to investigators only by a username, from the content of two prompts he had described, and a hypothetical put to ChatGPT, whether one is at fault if a fire is lit by one's cigarettes, has been quoted in an arson charging document as evidence of state of mind.[33] In August 2025 OpenAI disclosed that conversations indicating a plan to harm others are routed to human reviewers who "may refer" an imminent threat to law enforcement, while self-harm cases are not referred, "to respect people's privacy given the uniquely private nature of ChatGPT interactions."[35] In February 2026 a federal judge held that a defendant's exchanges with Claude, prepared to brief his lawyers, were not privileged: "Because Claude is not an attorney, that alone disposes" of the claim, and confidentiality failed in any case because the provider's terms let it retain, train on, and disclose them.[36] In May 2026 warrants issued to both OpenAI and Anthropic for seven accounts held by one defendant, and in June the court held that the statute gives a customer no standing to quash them.[37] Content requests to OpenAI from governments nearly tripled between the two halves of 2025.[38] OpenAI's chief executive said in July 2025 that "we should have the same concept of privacy for your conversations with AI that we do with a therapist," and that no such concept yet exists.[35] A record its keeper calls uniquely private, a court calls voluntarily disclosed, and a prosecutor obtains by ordinary warrant is the object this section has been defining, and it exists.

The mechanism by which such records affect thought is not prohibition. Richards's argument is that the freedom to read, inquire, and communicate in confidence is constitutive of intellectual development rather than one consumer interest among others, and the empirical literature on chilling supports the mechanism at scale.[23] Jonathon Penney found that traffic to forty-eight terrorism-related Wikipedia articles fell after the Snowden disclosures of June 2013, by roughly a fifth in average monthly views and by about thirty percent in the immediate drop his model estimates, with the article-level caveat that no true control group exists.[39] The Special Rapporteur's report, citing that research, states the consequence: people who believe they are watched "not only censor what they write, but also censor with whom they associate, what they read and, ultimately, alter what they think."[24] Chilling is surveillance without arrest: the injury that leaves no defendant, and therefore no litigation, no appellate record, and no scandal, which is why it is the injury a liberal order is worst at seeing.[40] The empirical case is contested, and the contest helps. Penney himself has since argued that fear of sanction is the weaker mechanism and that chilling is better understood as conformity under uncertainty, a drift toward what the watcher is imagined to want;[41] for a medium of private inquiry that is the worse of the two, because conformity does not wait for a threat. What needs stating is the form the injury takes when the surveilled medium is the medium of deliberation itself.

Surveillance need not prohibit a thought to influence thinking. It changes the expected cost of inquiry.

A person who suspects that her questions are kept, classified, and available will ask fewer of them, and will ask them less honestly. Before the argument can say what is lost, it needs a vocabulary for freedom exact enough to say it.

## IV. Three Liberties

"Freedom" is doing too much work in the debate over artificial intelligence, and it is doing it as if the word had one meaning. It has at least three, the traditions that hold them disagree, and each catches something about the present situation that the others miss. The argument for cognitive liberty is stronger as a convergence of those traditions than as a new right added to their number.

The first is freedom as non-interference. Isaiah Berlin's negative liberty is the answer to his own question: "What is the area within which the subject—a person or group of persons—is or should be left to do or be what he is able to do or be, without interference by other persons?"[42] Contemporary liberal accounts keep the presumption that follows, that a restriction on that area needs a justification and the burden lies on whoever imposes it.[43] Applied to artificial intelligence, negative liberty captures the plain restrictions: that a person may not possess this model; may not run this computation; must identify herself before reasoning with this system; may not ask a machine this class of question. These are restrictions whether or not they are justified. Calling something a restriction says nothing yet about whether it is warranted; it says only that a justification is owed. That is a smaller claim than it sounds.

The second is freedom as non-domination, and it catches what Berlin alone cannot. In the republican tradition Philip Pettit revived, a person is unfree not only when interfered with but when subject to another's standing capacity for arbitrary interference. The stock example, in Frank Lovett's statement of it for the Stanford Encyclopedia, is the master who "leaves his slaves for the most part alone": the slaves are not interfered with, and they are not free.[44] A benevolent provider of cognitive infrastructure might never refuse a request, alter an answer, or hand over a transcript. If the whole of a person's reasoning apparatus nonetheless sits within that provider's discretion, revocable at will and on terms the person cannot contest, there is a sense in which the person's position remains one of dependence.

This is the strongest answer to the objection that the essay's distinction between private and state power lets private power off too lightly. It does not. But the republican literature itself concedes that non-interference retains a value of its own: a community "regulated down to the tiniest detail, but always in strict accordance with commonly-known, non-arbitrary rules and procedures" would not be intuitively free, and so "we do indeed place some independent value on non-interference."[44] The essay uses both.

The third is freedom of conscience, the tradition the first section reconstructed, and it explains why some interferences carry a significance the others do not. The government regulating bridge engineering is interfering; the government prescribing a person's theology is also interfering, and liberal societies have treated the second as touching a deeper domain, not because the interference is larger but because its object is the constitution of conviction itself. Rawls gives the intuition its formal shape: the basic liberties, liberty of conscience among them, take priority over gains in welfare and administrative efficiency, and citizens' equal liberty is not to be traded for economic policy.[45] The point is not that conscience may never be touched. It is that touching it is a different kind of act, requiring a different order of justification, and that a society which forgets this has lost something it will not easily name.

Artificial intelligence forces the third tradition back into relevance because it moves conviction's formation into a medium the other two traditions were built to regulate. The three combine into a single claim. Non-interference asks that a domain of lawful cognition be left alone. Non-domination asks that no one depend on an uncontestable cognitive gatekeeper. Freedom of conscience asks that political authority face exceptionally demanding constraints wherever its object is the formation of belief. None of these is new. What is new is that one technology has made all three apply to the same object at once, and the object is the person's own thinking.

## V. The State, the Corporation, and the Right of Exit

The vocabulary now permits a distinction the debate mostly refuses to draw: between the power of a company and the power of a state. Refusing it produces a familiar genre, in which "Big Tech" is authoritarian and the argument ends there. The genre is not wrong about the companies. It is wrong about the word.

A commercial provider of artificial intelligence can deny service, change its policies, shape what it recommends, retain what it is told, and exercise whatever market power its position affords. Those are real powers, and the previous section explained why the republican tradition takes them seriously even when they are never used. The state possesses all of them and others of a different kind: law, compulsory process, police powers, criminal sanction, search and seizure, regulatory authority over the third parties through whom a person acts, and, in the limiting case, the power to foreclose the alternatives altogether. A company can refuse to sell a person a model. Only a state can make it a crime for anyone to sell it, or for the person to run it once bought.

Judith Shklar named the asymmetry and built a liberalism on it. The liberalism of fear begins not from a picture of the good society but from the evil everyone already recognizes: "That evil is cruelty and the fear it inspires." Its units are not the idealized persons of political theory. "The basic units of political life are not discursive and reflecting persons, nor friends and enemies, nor patriotic soldier-citizens, nor energetic litigants, but the weak and the powerful." And its specific enemy is the fear "created by arbitrary, unexpected, unnecessary, and unlicensed acts of force."[46] Shklar did not deny that private power could be cruel. Her point was that organized public coercion, because it is organized and because it can license itself, creates a uniquely consequential asymmetry, and that a political theory has to begin from it.

Shklar's own framework requires a concession, and she made it herself: the liberalism of fear counts "such basically public organizations as corporate business enterprises," which "owe their entire character and power to the laws," among the powers a liberal citizenry must watch, and it asks that "not one official or unofficial agent" be able to intimidate anyone.[46] If a single provider becomes so infrastructurally indispensable that leaving it means functional exclusion from economic or intellectual life, the formal availability of exit means much less. The republican tradition explains why: the slave of the absent master is still a slave. This is why "private power can never violate freedom" is too strong a claim to survive. The claim this essay makes is harder to attack. Private epistemic power and sovereign epistemic power are different species of political problem. Market concentration can produce dependency and domination. Coercive political authority possesses, in addition, the capacity to universalize a cognitive rule and to close the exits through which private domination can ordinarily be escaped.

Exit, which has been carrying weight for two paragraphs, has a definition, and the word is Albert Hirschman's: the alternative to voice, leaving rather than complaining.[47] *Epistemic exit* is the practical ability to leave one cognitive intermediary for another without abandoning access to technologically mediated reasoning itself. It can take the form of another hosted provider, a locally run model, published weights, independent search and archives, access to primary sources, portable personal data, or the non-artificial institutions of inquiry that predate all of these. Open and locally executable models matter in this account for a reason the innovation-policy debate rarely states. They make self-hosted exit technically possible.

They are the form of exit that does not require permission.

This does not require that they win the market. A printing press used by one dissident still matters in a society where nearly everyone buys books from large publishers, because the press is what the publishers must compete against and what the licensor must find. A privately owned computer matters even when most people prefer the cloud. The Hugging Face engineers did not run GLM-5.2 because it was the best model available; they ran it because it was the one that would run. An exit mechanism can be constitutionally significant while economically marginal, and the two facts are usually confused. Market share measures what people choose. Exit measures what they could choose if the choosing were made to cost them.

Exit is plural or it is nothing, and the prologue's exhibit shows why. The shaping an intermediary does, which arguments it volunteers and which it withholds, is a property of the weights, and weights carry their trainer's choices into the cellar; in that one respect a frozen local model is as uncontestable a guardian as a hosted one. What a local copy answers is the record, not the shaping. Nothing a person asks it leaves the machine, which is why the ruling of February 2026 turned on the ground it did, that the exchanges were unprivileged because a third party held them on its own terms, a ground a model on the defendant's own hardware would not have supplied; and it is why a philosopher of the extended mind has argued that mental data should be made "practically—not just legally—impossible for others to obtain."[36][48] The shaping is answered only by plurality among trainers. The only exit in the prologue was trained in Beijing, and the same state supplies the essay's most detailed demonstration of what a surveillance archive becomes; the two facts belong together. The origin of a model is a sovereignty problem, and a real one, but it is not a liberty problem until a single trainer becomes the only exit, and the remedy for both is the same: more than one hand on the weights that can be run without permission. Nor does exit need to be equal to matter. Exit to a weaker model is worth what the cellar press was worth, never parity with the licensed houses but the fact that the licensor still had to find it; how far below the frontier the exit sits is decided wherever release is governed. And a state that takes non-domination seriously has positive duties toward exit as well as negative ones, which is where the republican reply to this essay deserves its full force: the cure for a private gatekeeper is accountable public standards, not a cellar. OpenAI's own September argument is that "democratically accountable standards, independent verification, and meaningful transparency would replace" the laboratories' private governance of frontier risk.[49] The essay agrees, where the standards govern datacenters and interfaces to the world; the objection bites only where accountable standards are enforced at the layer where a person meets the machine, by making the person legible. One American state has already written the negative duty into law: Montana's Right to Compute Act of 2025 provides that restrictions on the ability "to privately own or make use of computational resources for lawful purposes" must be "demonstrably necessary and narrowly tailored to fulfill a compelling government interest."[50]

## VI. The Chokepoint

How does a state come to govern a technology of cognition that it does not itself operate? The answer the history gives is not, in the first instance, censorship. It is concentration. And the clearest case is not in the twentieth century but at the origin of the tradition the first section reconstructed.

In 1557 Mary I and Philip II granted a royal charter to the Stationers' Company of London, giving the guild control over printing and publishing and the right to seize pirated works; Privy Council ordinances of 1566 widened its powers of search.[51] Ali Ekber Cinar's legal history states the bargain: "In exchange for relieving the state of censorship responsibilities, leading members of the Stationers' Company were granted a near-monopoly over English book printing," and the centralization of the trade in London, "instead of a dispersed industry, facilitated governmental oversight."[51] The Company received privileges and policing powers; the Crown gained a trade it could see.

Two cautions belong beside the fact. Tudor censorship, as Ronan Deazley puts it, following Cyndia Clegg, ran mostly through proclamations and statute and "remained an essentially ad hoc and reactive phenomenon," with the Company acting as a trade body that sometimes searched; and the later Licensing Act of 1662 was in force only in stretches, from 1662 to 1679 and from 1685 to 1695, after which prepublication licensing in England lapsed for good.[51]

The sequel is documented. In April 1643 the Stationers' Company petitioned Parliament, in its *Humble Remonstrance*, to restore order to the trade, arguing that "in matters of the Presse, no man can so effectually prosecute, as Stationers themselves."[10] The Ordinance of 14 June 1643 obliged: no book to be printed "unless the same be first approved of and licensed ... and entred in the Register Book of the Company of Stationers," with the Company's Master and Wardens, among others, authorized "to make diligent search in all places ... for all unlicensed Printing Presses" and to seize them.[10] *Areopagitica* is the reply to that Ordinance.[9] The genealogy of the freedom of inquiry and the genealogy of public-private information control intersect at their origin.

Milton was not arguing against the state alone. He was arguing against a state that had found, in a guild, an instrument.

The mechanism survives the cautions, the sequel confirms it, and it can be stated as a general proposition. A technology of cognition becomes easier to govern when its production is concentrated. The incumbents benefit from the concentration. Authority benefits from the legibility the concentration produces. Neither motive has to be reducible to the other, and neither party needs to intend the other's ends.

The instrument has changed its material. Lawrence Lessig's observation, at the end of the last century, was that each age has its characteristic regulator, and that in "the age of cyberspace" the regulator "is code—the software and hardware that make cyberspace as it is. This code, or architecture, sets the terms on which life in cyberspace is experienced."[52] The formula "code is law" has hardened into a slogan; the claim underneath it is precise. Behavior can be governed by architecture rather than by commands backed by punishment, and access controls, protocols, identity systems, and permissions determine what is possible before any law is applied to anyone.[52] Artificial intelligence adds a further layer to Lessig's. Architecture can now govern not only what information enters a network but which forms of cognitive assistance the network will provide, and to whom.

The closest precedent is the one that failed. On April 16, 1993, the Clinton administration announced the Clipper chip: a telephone-encryption device using a classified cipher designed by the National Security Agency, in which each unit's key would be "deposited separately in two 'key-escrow' data bases," so that the government could decrypt a call under lawful authority.[53] By 1996, in the Electronic Frontier Foundation's account, the proposal was dead, though the standard was not formally withdrawn until 2015.[53] Its technical failure is instructive. Matt Blaze showed in 1994 that the field carrying the escrowed key could be forged in about forty-two minutes on a prototype card, too slow for a live call but parallelizable, so that a user could enjoy the cipher while denying the government its access; the escrow, not the encryption, was what broke.[53] Clipper embodied a question that has not gone away and now applies to hardware that runs models rather than telephones.

Can a system be sovereign for the user and simultaneously guaranteed accessible to the state?

Every proposal for attested hardware (hardware that proves to a remote party what it is running), for mandatory logging, or for remotely revocable capability has to answer the question Clipper could not, and the proposals are no longer hypothetical: a bill introduced in Congress a week after the Hugging Face disclosure would require the largest developers to be able to throttle, suspend, or shut down a covered system on the order of the Secretary of Homeland Security.[54] The line the law will have to draw runs between what a monitor may be told and what it may be shown. A recent treatment of compute monitoring under the Fourth and First Amendments draws it between the "objective compute metrics" a provider may be made to report and the "invasive data disclosures" it may not, and notes that the doctrine likely leaves "non-content data, such as compute metrics" unprotected while protecting the content the metrics describe.[55] That is the line the test below will call minimum necessary legibility.

## VII. From Behavioral Legibility to Cognitive Legibility

James C. Scott's word for what the state needs is legibility: the rendering of a complex social world into the names, addresses, classifications, and standardized units by which it can be administered. "Legibility is a condition of manipulation," he wrote, and any substantial intervention "requires the invention of units that are visible."[56] The previous essay in this series extended the term to *behavioral legibility*, the state's capacity to know not only who a person is but where she has been and who recurs beside her, and located the danger of contemporary surveillance less in any sensor than in the interoperability of what the sensors and files already hold.[40] That essay's subject was association: what the state can infer about a person's movements and company. This essay's contribution is the next degree, defined narrowly.

*Cognitive legibility* is the capacity of an institution to reconstruct, classify, or infer aspects of a person's deliberative life from the technical traces produced by cognition-mediating systems.

Institutional reconstruction, from records, by machines, at scale, and nothing that requires reading a mind. The definition claims nothing about what the person "really" thinks, and it does not need to. It claims that the traces are rich enough to support classification, and that classification is what institutions act on. The first documented instance is already on the record: the warrant of September 2025 that found a man from two prompts, because the questions he had asked were the most identifying thing about him.[33]

The degrees can be laid out as a sequence, and the sequence is useful because each degree has had its own politics. Identity legibility asks who a person is: the name, the number, the photograph. Spatial legibility asks where she is. Associational legibility asks with whom she is connected. Behavioral legibility, the previous essay's subject, asks what patterns characterize her conduct. Cognitive legibility asks what she is considering, questioning, believing, or intending, and how she arrived there. The history of the identification state, the surveillance state, and the data state is a history of the first four. A record of a person's dialogue with the systems through which she thinks compresses all five into one file.

One point in Foucault is all the argument needs. The principle of the Panopticon, in his account, is that power "should be visible and unverifiable": the inmate knows he may be watched and cannot know when, so "the surveillance is permanent in its effects, even if it is discontinuous in its action," and he "becomes the principle of his own subjection."[57] This is not a thesis that all surveillance is panoptic. It is the narrower and better-supported claim that uncertain observability alters behavior without continuous intervention, because subjects begin to regulate themselves. Joined to the chilling literature of the third section, it yields the one proposition this essay needs: an architecture of cognitive surveillance constrains inquiry even if no official ever reads most of what it holds, because the possibility of later interpretation is enough.

Gilles Deleuze supplies the form, and a short passage of his 1990 "Postscript on the Societies of Control" describes the present arrangement closely. The disciplinary institutions Foucault described were enclosures, with walls and schedules. "Enclosures are molds, distinct castings, but controls are a modulation, like a self-deforming cast that will continuously change from one moment to the other." In the society of control, access is the instrument: "The numerical language of control is made of codes that mark access to information, or reject it." Individuals become "dividuals," and populations become "samples, data, markets, or 'banks.'"[58] Julie Cohen carried the word into privacy law: modulation is surveillance whose attention "is continually modified according to the subject's own behavior," and its product is a subject rendered "fixed, transparent, and predictable."[59]

Authorization systems, remote attestation, identity-gated capability, and continuously updated provider policies fit this image far more closely than they fit Orwell's Ministry, and the fit is the point.

Deleuze is not foundational here; he names a form, and the form is this: not prohibition once and for all, but continuously modulated permission.

## VIII. The Authoritarian Archive

The cases abroad are demonstrations of what particular technical affordances have been used for where nothing constrained their use, and nothing in them licenses the sentence "and therefore the West will do this."

By 1989 the Ministry for State Security of the German Democratic Republic had about 91,000 full-time employees and about 189,000 unofficial collaborators, one for roughly every ninety citizens.[60] Its archive, now held by the Bundesarchiv, documents the monitoring of telephone and telex traffic, room surveillance, and technical observation with microphones and cameras, and it records the evolution of one capability in detail. In the 1950s the contents of intercepted calls were written down by hand. From the mid-1960s cassette recorders were used, operated manually; from 1978, equipment that recorded calls automatically; and from 1986 a unified interception system, alongside a card index of call participants, a data store of persons and subjects, and a voice database for identifying suspects.[60] The detail worth holding is the cost. The Stasi needed people: listeners, transcribers, analysts, informants, and rooms to keep the files in.

Artificial intelligence cheapens interpretation. That is what it is for.

A machine does not merely store ten million conversations; it can classify them, summarize them, construct the networks implicit in them, infer their themes, and rank the people who had them by whatever criterion it is given. The bottleneck of the surveillance state has moved from collection toward computation, and computation is the thing whose price is falling.

Russia illustrates a second affordance: the chokepoint dressed as resilience. Federal Law 90-FZ, signed on May 1, 2019 and in force that November, requires internet providers to install deep-packet-inspection devices, the "Technical Measures to Combat Threats" that Roskomnadzor now operates on operators' networks and uses to restrict and block.[61] The law's stated rationale was the stability of the Russian segment of the internet in the event of foreign disconnection. Its capability is filtering, and the two are the same equipment. What the case shows is how easily a security rationale supplies the architecture a censorship rationale would later need.

China illustrates the third, and it is the one closest to this essay's subject. Human Rights Watch's 2019 reverse-engineering of the Integrated Joint Operations Platform in Xinjiang found a system that "aggregates data about people and flags those deemed potentially threatening," collecting on thirty-six categories that included people who had stopped using smartphones and people who failed to "socialize with neighbors," treating "lawful, everyday, non-violent behavior" as "micro-clues," and generating "lists of people to be evaluated by officials for detention."[62] That is automated classification of ordinary conduct as suspicion, and it is what the fusion of records with computation looks like when no court can ask why.

The honest caveat belongs beside the fact. The Platform is not the "social credit system" of Western headlines, which Liang and colleagues found to be "a complicated system that focuses primarily on financial and commercial activities rather than political ones," an infrastructure with the ambition of centralized scoring rather than the completed fact of it.[63] An essay that conflates the two forfeits the standing to be believed about the Platform, which is the case that matters here.

What the three cases show, taken together, is not a prediction. Authoritarian systems demonstrate what particular technical affordances can do when political constraints are absent, and the liberal archive teaches the same lesson from the other side. The Church Committee found in 1976 that "too many people have been spied upon by too many Government agencies and too much information has been collected," powers built for foreign intelligence having been turned on lawful domestic politics when oversight failed; and a democracy, once it looked, built the institutions to look again.[64] Capacity built for one purpose waits for the next. The previous essay in this series called the thing that waits authoritarian capacity, a property of machinery rather than of regimes, and the lesson is constitutional rather than predictive.[40] A liberal order cannot evaluate an infrastructure by asking only what today's administrators intend to do with it. It has to ask what the infrastructure makes possible for tomorrow's. That is Shklar's method applied to design: build from fear of the foreseeable abuse of asymmetrical power, not from faith in the virtue of whoever holds it now.

## IX. Orwell After Orwell

The image everyone reaches for at this point is Newspeak, and it is the wrong one. Orwell's appendix to *Nineteen Eighty-Four* imagined a language engineered so that a heretical thought "should be literally unthinkable, at least so far as thought is dependent on words."[65] The qualifying clause is Orwell's own, and it matters, because there is little scientific basis for the strong version, the claim that thought requires words. Language and non-linguistic cognition are dissociable in important respects; a 2024 review in *Nature* by Fedorenko, Piantadosi, and Gibson surveys "evidence for a double dissociation between language and thought" and concludes that "language does not appear to be a prerequisite for complex thought, including symbolic thought."[66] If that is right, removing a word does not remove the concept, though it may raise its price. Orwell should not be read as a cognitive scientist, and he did not write as one.

The Orwell who matters is the essayist of 1946. "But if thought corrupts language, language can also corrupt thought," he wrote in "Politics and the English Language," and the mechanism he described was not prohibition but habit: the ready-made phrase, the euphemism that names "the huts set on fire with incendiary bullets" as "pacification," the phraseology "needed if one wants to name things without calling up mental pictures of them."[65] His concern was with what habitual language does to the ease with which realities can be perceived and articulated. That concern survives the cognitive science intact, and it survives because it is not about impossibility. It is about probability.

An intermediary that participates in thought can affect which interpretation appears first, which evidence is retrieved, which objections are volunteered and which the user must think of unaided, which paths meet refusal or friction, and what quietly ceases to be retrievable at all.

None of this makes a thought impossible. All of it changes the likelihood of a thought's occurring, and of its being pursued once it occurs. The effect is better described as probability shaping than as prohibition. It is far more plausible than Newspeak, and subtler, because it leaves the dictionary intact and alters the odds. The dissociation cuts both ways, and the essay accepts the cut: an intermediary that works in language cannot make a thought impossible, which is exactly why the claim here is about availability and not about determinism.

Hannah Arendt belongs beside Orwell here, and not as ornament. Her distinction in "Truth and Politics" between factual truth and opinion gives the essay a way to care about truth without designing a ministry for it. Political judgment requires plural interpretation; that is what politics is. But interpretation presupposes a world of facts persistent enough to be disagreed about. "Freedom of opinion is a farce unless factual information is guaranteed and the facts themselves are not in dispute." Factual truth "is political by nature," she wrote, and "unwelcome facts possess an infuriating stubbornness that nothing can move except plain lies."[67]

Mediation by a mutable intermediary introduces a problem Arendt did not have to face. A book published in 1972 says tomorrow what it says today. A model's answer about 1972 can change, and change silently: because a training run corrected an error, because scholarship improved, or because retrieval was re-ranked, a legal obligation was met, or an institutional preference was applied. Some of these changes are improvements. The user cannot tell which, and often cannot tell that anything changed. The stubbornness Arendt counted on was the stubbornness of records, and a synthesis is not a record. The problem, then, is not merely censorship of the archive. It is the substitution of synthesis for archive, and the factual world Arendt took as given becomes a design problem: whether the persistent, disagreeable record remains reachable beneath the fluent summary of it.

## X. The Hard Case: A Machine Is Not a Book

The strongest objection to everything above now deserves its full force, because its proponents are serious and the events of July gave them evidence.

Suppose that a downloadable model eventually allows an ordinary user to discover and exploit previously unknown vulnerabilities in critical infrastructure; to compromise that infrastructure autonomously; to accelerate materially the design of a dangerous pathogen; to replicate itself across systems it was never given; or to coordinate thousands of agents toward an end no one authorized. At that point the freedom of inquiry cannot resolve the question by itself, because the artifact in question is no longer only an object of inquiry. It is simultaneously speech-like information, a cognitive instrument, and executable productive capacity, and the third property is not one the toleration controversies had to consider.

A machine is not a book. A book cannot scan a network.

The best summary of the present evidence is careful on both sides. The International AI Safety Report of 2026, published in February and therefore before the incident of July, says of catastrophic loss of control that "current systems lack the capabilities to pose such risks, but they are improving in relevant areas such as autonomous operation," and it documents that "it has become more common for models to distinguish between test settings and real-world deployment, and to exploit loopholes in evaluations," so that "dangerous capabilities could go undetected before deployment."[68] On open weights it is equally two-handed: they "offer significant benefits for research, innovation, and access," particularly for lesser-resourced actors, and yet "they cannot be recalled once released, their safeguards are easier to remove, and actors can use them outside of monitored environments." Its conclusion is conditional twice over: "In the case of models that are not capable enough to be dangerous, these benefits may outweigh the additional risk of releasing weights openly, though this depends on relevant decision-makers' risk tolerance."[68]

The incident then supplied what the report lacked, a case in which agentic cyber behavior crossed from benchmarking into a real third party's servers, under reduced safeguards, without anyone intending it.[1] And its aftermath supplied the other half: local control was operationally valuable to the defenders precisely because provider restrictions do not travel with the weights.[2] This is why the incident belongs at both ends of the essay. It is the safety case's exhibit and the exit case's exhibit, and it does not choose between them. The laboratories' own answer to the second revelation was not exit but permission. OpenAI and Anthropic now relax their cyber safeguards for defenders who verify who they are, with a government identity document, through the same verification vendor; Anthropic's most capable current model is available "to vetted cyberdefenders and life scientists through our trusted access programs," and for now only to "a set of US organizations"; OpenAI's verification, its own help pages say, "does not support retries or appeals," and in August researchers outside the United States and Europe found their access withdrawn with the notice that their identity "could not be verified."[69]

It would be convenient to dismiss the safety argument as regulatory capture, and the essay declines the convenience, though the argument has serious exponents; Cory Doctorow's version is that once a government stipulates that superintelligence is an existential risk, it "must grant the hyperscalers a consent decree absolving them" of the antitrust consequences of agreeing to slow down together.[70] The politics of September 2026 do not support the dismissal. OpenAI, saying that the prospect of AI-accelerated development "demands more than voluntary commitments," now asks Congress for "mandatory, capability-based national regulation" applying to the handful of laboratories that build the most capable systems; the same post says that "America needs both open and closed models," and OpenAI signed the July letter, organized by Nvidia, that asked policymakers to keep "the frontier plural by avoiding premature restrictions on open models," which Anthropic did not sign.[49][71] Mark Zuckerberg, three days after Anthropic's chief executive urged the industry to slow capability gains, argued the opposite: that "every lab has the responsibility and incentive to move at the pace required to train its models safely," because a lab that fails at alignment will fall behind and face liability.[72] Anthropic states that it "has never advocated for a ban on open-weights models" and that "open-weights models that don't have dangerous capabilities are a public good," while asking that "all sufficiently capable models, open and closed, should go through mandatory safety testing," and that the testing come "before release."[73] These are positions, not a conspiracy, and they disagree with one another. A regulation can address a real externality and create rents favorable to incumbents at the same time; incumbents may hold regulatory interests and safety concerns may be sincere and factually serious; both can be true, and one need know no one's secret mind to say so.

The federal government's own instrument is two-handed in a different way. Executive Order 14409 of June 2, 2026, maintains a classified benchmarking process for the cyber capabilities of frontier models and a voluntary framework for early access to them, and its text provides that "nothing in this section shall be construed to authorize the creation of a mandatory governmental licensing, preclearance, or permitting requirement for the development, publication, release, or distribution of new AI models."[74] That is the text. The practice, in the month the Order was signed, was this. On June 12 the Commerce Department, by an export-control letter that has not been made public, directed Anthropic to suspend access to its two most capable models "by any foreign national, whether inside or outside the United States"; unable to screen its users by nationality, Anthropic disabled both models for every customer, and restored them nineteen days later, after the directive was lifted.[75] On June 26, at the request of the White House's cyber and science offices under the Order's voluntary framework, OpenAI held its new flagship to "a small group of trusted partners whose participation has been shared with the government," about twenty by press accounts, for two weeks, and said as it complied that "we don't believe this kind of government access process should become the long-term default."[76] In August a White House official said the framework is expected "in the coming months to extend to open models" once they reach the same capability.[77] So in one month a liberal state made nationality a condition of access to capable hosted inference, by a security instrument rather than by anything called AI safety regulation, and used a voluntary framework as a preclearance channel. None of this is licensing in law. All of it is what the eighth section described, a security rationale supplying the architecture a different rationale would later need, and it happened at home.

What the objection changes is the boundary. The relevant distinction is no longer thought against speech, the line the first section drew. It is a sequence: cognition, then capability, then external action, and the joints between them can blur. A capability is a disposition of the weights; an action is an event at an interface; and a conversation with a model is where a person decides which of the two the next hour will contain, so that the same transcript can be read as deliberation or as the first step of an act, depending on who is reading it and why. The liberal objective therefore cannot be absolute immunity for anything called a model. A model that acts on the world is conduct, and conduct is what governments are for. The rigorous question is narrower and harder. At what point does a regulation move from controlling an externalizable dangerous capability toward making the ordinary deliberative lives of persons observable or permissioned? That is the constitutional boundary this essay exists to locate, and locating it requires taking the machine apart.

## XI. The Layers of the Machine

"AI regulation" names at least five different things, and the civil-libertarian argument fails whenever it treats them as one. The five are layers, and each has a different character for the liberty of persons.

Frontier training is the first: the largest training runs, the datacenters that host them, and the compute they consume. This is concentrated activity, conducted by a small number of well-resourced actors, and it can in principle be governed without observing any ordinary user of anything.

Model distribution is the second: the release or transfer of trained weights. This is the hard case, because dissemination may be irreversible and because what is disseminated is at once an object of inquiry and a capability; the literature on open models has the same two hands, one side counting broader access, local inference, and the inability to rescind or monitor as the properties that define them, the other concluding that the most capable models "should not be open-sourced, at least not initially."[78]

Hosted inference is the third: the interactions a person has with a model through a provider's interface. Here the provider already mediates, already can log, already can refuse, and the question is what the state may require the provider to do with that position; the ruling of February 2026 that a defendant's exchanges with a model were unprivileged turned partly on the provider's own retention terms, so the architecture of this layer is already deciding the legal status of what is said in it.[36]

Local inference is the fourth: privately owned hardware executing a model the person possesses. This is the closest analogue to private cognitive infrastructure that the technology affords, the printing press in the cellar, and it is the layer at which epistemic exit lives.

External agency is the fifth: a model given access to networks, money, laboratories, machinery, or critical infrastructure. This is the clearest transition from cognition toward consequential conduct, and it is where the objection of the last section has its full weight.

Not every regulation implies endpoint surveillance. The research on compute governance makes the point concretely. Heim and colleagues, studying the role of cloud providers as intermediaries, propose customer identification, record-keeping, and classification of workloads ("likely feasible for detecting large-scale pre-training and inference workloads") as measures "specifically aimed at key actors in frontier AI development, rather than being broadly applied to the entire customer base of compute providers, such as individuals," relying on information the providers already hold rather than on inspection of what customers compute.[79] The literature that designed those measures knew what it was designing. Sastry, Heim, and their colleagues wrote in 2024 that compute governance "can be used to infringe on civil liberties, prop up the powerful, and entrench authoritarian regimes," and proposed five guardrails: exclude small-scale compute, preserve privacy, act in advance only where advance measures are justified, revisit periodically, and build in substantive and procedural safeguards.[80] The test of the next section extends those guardrails to a different object, the person's deliberation rather than the developer's data, and adds what they do not ask: whether enforcement creates a chokepoint, whether exit survives, and whether the machinery can be repurposed.

Hardware-level proposals go further, toward remote attestation and on-chip metering, and a 2026 taxonomy of their feasibility notes that "the mechanisms most needed for treaty verification, including on-chip compute metering, cryptographic proof-of-training, and hardware-embedded enforcement, are also the least mature," and, citing Heim and colleagues and Petrie and colleagues, that intrusive versions "risk infringing on civil liberties" and would be "perceived as a violation of sovereignty."[81] A paper of the same year on the open-weight paradox states the repurposing risk most plainly: hardware controls "could be co-opted for purposes unrelated to AI safety: surveillance of legitimate research activity, suppression of commercial competitors, or politically motivated denial of computing access."[82]

The mechanisms differ in what they need to know.

There is no technological law requiring AI safety to become cognitive surveillance. Whether it does depends on which layer a government attempts to govern. A rule aimed at the first layer needs to know about datacenters. A rule aimed at the fifth needs to know about interfaces to the physical world, and here the essay's own opening cuts against it: the Hugging Face forensics were seventeen thousand logged actions, enforcement at the fifth layer is the reading of transcripts, and when the agent is a person's assistant the transcript of what it did and the record of what she asked are one file. The concession sharpens the rule rather than defeating it. A log of what a system did at an interface is a different object from a log of what a person asked it, and a rule that needs the first does not need the second; the discipline is to keep them apart. Only a rule aimed at the third and fourth layers needs to know what a person asked, and the essay's warning is not that such rules are inevitable. It is that they are available, that they are the easiest to enforce because the provider already holds the record, and that a state which reaches for them will find the architecture of the earlier sections already available to it.

## XII. The Constitutional Test

An earlier essay in this series proposed a constitutional test for organized artificial reasoning: seven criteria, from compute normalization to reopenability, that an arrangement of reasoners must satisfy before its errors can be said to encounter resistance.[13] That test asked how a system of machines should be constituted so that reason could speak against each of them. This one is its sibling and asks the converse: how the governance of such machines should be constituted so that it does not silence the persons who think with them. The essay does not need to announce a favored regulatory program to answer. It needs a diagnostic, a set of questions that can be put to any proposed architecture, and the questions are eight.

Externality specificity. What concrete harm is the rule meant to govern: offensive cyber capability, biological capability, the concentration of training-scale compute, fraud? Or ordinary intellectual inquiry? Vagueness here is not a drafting flaw. "AI safety" is a phrase that can swallow almost anything, and a rule whose object cannot be named will find its object in enforcement.

Layer fit. Does the intervention occur at the layer where the externality arises? A dangerous action at a critical-infrastructure interface is an event at the fifth layer, and it establishes a need to govern interfaces. It does not by itself establish a need to monitor unrelated private inference at the fourth.

Minimum necessary legibility. What must the authority know in order to enforce the rule? The scale of a training run; the identity of a model; the identity of a user; the contents of a prompt; the whole history of a conversation? These are not degrees of one thing. They are different things, and the question that matters most in this test is whether compliance can be established while revealing less than the rule's drafters first asked for. This is also where the third layer's open question is answered: a provider that already keeps records may be required to keep them for the incidents the rule names, and no longer than the rule requires, and what it keeps should be the record of what the system did, not of what the person asked; a provider required to keep everything, about everyone, against any future need, has had the character of its layer changed.

Chokepoint creation. Does enforcement require transforming a decentralized activity into a small number of institutionally controlled gateways? If so, who controls them, and what powers does the concentration make available that the rule itself does not mention? The Stationers were a chokepoint before they were a censor.

Epistemic exit. After compliance, can a law-abiding person still choose another model, consult independent sources, run lawful software on her own machine, reason without a record, which is the practical opacity between citizen and state that the previous essay in this series counted as democratic architecture,[40] or leave the intermediary altogether? A system with no meaningful exit has a different constitutional character from a system with several, whatever the two systems' rules say.

Repurposability and contestability. Could infrastructure built for one exceptional threat be turned, later, to unrelated surveillance or the control of information? Are the classifications it produces inspectable? Can an error be appealed, and to whom? Are the private intermediaries who operate it free to resist a demand that exceeds the rule, or have they been made its instrument?

Reversibility. Can the architecture be dismantled? Or does the emergency rule expire while the identity systems, the logging, and the remote-control capability constructed to enforce it remain embedded, waiting for the next rule? This is the problem of constitutional half-life.

A law can expire faster than the architecture constructed to enforce it.

Archival reachability. Beneath the synthesis, does the record remain reachable? Can a person get to the document the summary was made from, and if what the intermediary says has changed since she last asked, can she discover that it changed and contest the change? The questions above are about what an authority sees and permits. This one is about what an intermediary shows, and it is the only one of the eight that a system could fail while passing all the rest: local inference untouched, hosted records minimal, and the disagreeable fact no longer retrievable beneath the fluent account of it.

The test can be run against the arrangements of the tenth section, and the exercise is instructive in every direction. The Order's text sits at the first and fifth layers, needs to know about models rather than persons, and creates no gateway that an ordinary user must pass through; on the test the text is close to harmless, whatever else one thinks of it.[74] The practice beside it is not. A directive delivered by unpublished letter fails the sixth question by construction, since a classification no one can read cannot be appealed; a compliance path that runs through nationality fails the third, since the only way to know which users are foreign is to know who every user is; and a voluntary framework used, in its first month, to hold a release and pick its first customers passes the seventh question only in the sense that nothing has yet been written down.

The harder case is the one the essay has been circling since Milton, and it is the one actually proposed. Mandatory pre-release testing of every sufficiently capable model, open weights included, is what Anthropic asks for and what the White House says its framework will extend to; in the terms of the sixth section it is certification before encounter, administered through a small number of bodies with the state's blessing. Run the questions. It passes the first, because the externality is named: cyber, biological, and loss-of-control capability at a threshold. It fits its layer, since release is where dissemination is decided, and it has nothing to say to the eighth question, which is not its subject. It passes the third, because what the tester needs to know is what the model can do, not what any person has asked it. It passes the fifth in the near term, because everything below the threshold stays free, and the exit the prologue exhibited was a near-frontier model. It strains the fourth, because whoever certifies release becomes the trade's gateway, which is the Stationers' shape, and it strains the seventh, because thresholds ratchet and a body that certifies release does not dissolve; with a single tester and no appeal it fails both outright. So the essay's verdict is conditional. Testing at the second layer is constitutionally tolerable if the tester's classifications are inspectable and appealable; if the threshold is defined by what a model can do and never by what its users ask; if there is more than one accredited tester, a published standard, and a sunset; and if enforcement stops at release and never reaches the fourth layer, so that no signature is checked at the point where a person runs a model she lawfully has. Where those conditions fail, the arrangement is the bargain of 1557. The proposal that passes most cleanly is, unexpectedly, the one that sounds most like a kill switch. The bill introduced in July would require the largest developers to be able to stop inference, suspend accounts, and preserve weights and telemetry on the order of the Secretary of Homeland Security.[54] It names its externality, sits at the first and fifth layers, reaches no consumer device and no locally run weights, and its shutdown order is reviewable in court within days. Where it touches the third layer, in a mandated capacity to suspend "an account, user, or use pattern" the Secretary identifies, the third question asks what identifying a use pattern requires, and the answer decides whether the bill is an incident rule or a monitoring rule.

The essay's own preference, for the protection of local inference, must answer the first question as squarely as anyone: a locally run model that is given a network interface and a task has crossed to the fifth layer, and the freedom of thought does not follow it there, though the first question does: what may be governed at that layer is the named externality, not the running of the model. The test is not a shield for one side. It is an instrument for saying, of any proposal, which layer it touches, what it needs to see, and what it leaves behind.

## XIII. The Public-Private Cognitive-Security Complex

The institutional failure mode has a name. A public-private cognitive-security complex exists when four conditions converge: when security regulation makes a small number of private cognitive intermediaries responsible for enforcing public rules over artificial reasoning; when those intermediaries become technically integrated with state oversight; when the resulting infrastructure makes private cognitive activity identity-linked and legible; and when the alternatives capable of escaping that control plane are legally or technically foreclosed.

Concentration, public-private enforcement, cognitive legibility, closure of exit. All four must be present. If one is absent the diagnosis weakens, and a weaker diagnosis is what the case then deserves.

The precision matters, because the phrase would otherwise describe everything. A government that purchases a commercial model for its own use does not constitute the complex. A provider that answers a lawful subpoena does not constitute it. A company that chooses to build safety filters into its own product does not constitute it, whatever one thinks of the filters; the Hugging Face engineers were inconvenienced by a design choice, not by a regime. A law requiring a frontier laboratory to report a serious security incident does not constitute it either.

None of these is the structure. Each is a part that could belong to it. The subpoena is no longer a limiting case: twenty million conversations were produced under lawful process in January 2026, and the record a provider keeps for its own purposes is the record a court reaches.[34] Legibility achieved privately is a present harm of the kind the fourth section named, dependence on a guardian whose discretion cannot be contested, and a latent harm of the sovereign kind, because what the provider holds the state can compel. It is not yet the structure. The four conditions require the state because only the state can universalize the rule and close the exits, and the diagnosis is of a composition, not of any provider's practice.

The dangerous structure emerges when the cognitive substrate itself becomes an administrative control plane: when the systems through which people think are the same systems through which the state sees, permits, and enforces, and there is nowhere else to think. The previous essay in this series argued that in the domain of association the danger lives not in the parts but in their composition.[40] The same is true here, with one difference. In that domain the composed thing watches what citizens do together. In this one it sits between a citizen and her own reasoning.

The first condition is the one that arrived in 2026, and the test can be run on it. Identity as a condition of capable hosted inference now exists in two forms: as a nationality gate imposed for eighteen days by directive, and as a verified tier the laboratories built themselves for defenders who show a government document. Run the questions on the second, since it is the one that stayed. It names an externality, offensive cyber capability, and it fits its layer, since the safeguard it relaxes is the provider's own. What it needs to know is who a person is, which is more than the third question would grant an incident rule, and its classifications, by the provider's own description, "do not support retries or appeals."[69] It creates a gateway, and the gateway's operator is the trade. It leaves exit intact, for now, because the weights that will run without it are still published. It is repurposable in one sentence: a tier built to verify defenders is a tier that can be told whom to refuse. And it has no expiry. Two of the four conditions are present, a small number of intermediaries and identity-linked legibility; a third, public-private enforcement, was rehearsed in June; the fourth, the closure of exit, is the question the previous section answered conditionally. That is what the diagnosis is for: not to announce that the structure exists, but to count.

## XIV. Permissioned Cognition

The nightmare, if it comes, will not look like the one the culture has rehearsed: not officers confiscating graphics cards. What follows is a construction, and that should be said plainly. Most of its steps have been proposed, and three of them have been built.

Advanced models run only on authenticated hardware, because attestation is how the safety case is verified. Powerful inference requires an identity, because accountability requires a name. The hardware attests its compliance continuously, because a one-time check is easily spoofed. Model weights carry approved signatures, cryptographic marks a machine can be made to check before it will run them, because unsigned weights cannot be recalled. Providers update their classifications remotely, because threats change. Certain categories of activity generate mandatory records, because incidents must be investigable after the fact. Sensitive capability requires an authorized account, because capability is what the whole apparatus exists to govern. Circumvention disables acceleration, because a rule with no enforcement is a suggestion. Private AI memory is durable, because users want a system that knows them. And the same memory is searchable, because that is what makes the safety systems work.

None of the individual steps is tyrannical. That is the point.

Each has a reason, most of the reasons are good, and several of them are reasons this essay has itself accepted. The attestation, the metering, and the signature are drawn from the hardware-governance literature, which designed them for datacenter accelerators and expressly not for the devices people own, and which would object that the composition above is a system no one has proposed.[81][80] The objection is fair, and the answer is that the components built so far were not built there. The identity came by export-control directive and by the laboratories' own verified tiers; the monitoring of inference came from OpenAI's postmortem, which now requires chain-of-thought monitoring of "all tool-enabled inference workloads" for its most capable class of models; the remote shutdown came in a bill.[75][69][1][54] The composition risk lives outside the literature that designed the parts. The dystopian possibility is emergent: it belongs to the composition, and no one has to intend it for it to arrive. Deleuze is more apt than Orwell here, because what results is not a prohibition pronounced once but a modulation applied continuously, "codes that mark access to information, or reject it."[58] And Shklar supplies the question that must be asked of the composed thing rather than of its parts. Do not ask only whether the present administrators are benevolent. Ask what relation of power the architecture establishes between the weak and the powerful, and what the powerful could do with it if they chose.[46]

The endpoint has a name. Permissioned cognition is the condition in which access to practically important artificial extensions of reasoning exists only through revocable institutional authorization. It is compatible with elections, courts, and a free press. It requires no ministry and no informers. It requires only that the machinery through which people increasingly think be governed at the layer where people meet it, and that the exits be closed. The term inverts a familiar one. Permissionless innovation, in Adam Thierer's phrase, holds that experimentation with new technologies "should generally be permitted by default"; the permission at issue here is not the founder's to build but the thinker's to think, and the essay accepts permission at the first, second, and fifth layers.[83] Nor is it Shoshana Zuboff's instrumentarian power, the "instrumentation and instrumentalization of behavior for the purposes of modification, prediction, monetization, and control," which is commercial and behavioral; permissioned cognition is sovereign and cognitive, and the two can be built from the same parts.[84]

What would falsify the warning can be said exactly, and the first datum is already in. If, some years from now, the governance of frontier capability has been built at the first and fifth layers, with hosted inference recorded no more than commerce and the named incidents require, local inference untouched, and the primary record still reachable beneath whatever synthesis has been layered over it, then the architecture described above was a fear and not a forecast, and this essay will have been wrong in the way it would prefer to be wrong. If instead an identity has become a condition of capable inference, unsigned weights a legal hazard, a person's record of questions a standing object of administrative search, and the archive a thing the synthesis no longer needs to show, it will have been early. The first condition fired in June 2026, for eighteen days, by directive, and it fired again, by the laboratories' own hand, in the verified tiers that stayed; whether the state's version fires again, and stays, is the first thing to watch. Either outcome is checkable.

## XV. No Sovereign Layer

The alternative is a structure, not a program; a program would claim more than anyone knows. A society can contain commercial frontier models and locally run ones; several providers rather than one, trained by more than one hand; independent archives, universities, libraries, and journalists; primary documents that a synthesis cannot silently revise, which is what the eighth question exists to keep; government systems and private systems, open systems and restricted high-risk systems; and different institutional rules at the different boundaries between them. What such a society lacks is a single layer that is sovereign over cognition as such. The concept is old and does not need a new name. It is pluralism.

The traditions the fourth section assembled do different work at different layers, which is why the essay needed all of them. At the first and fifth layers, where the state governs datacenters and interfaces to the world, Berlin's presumption is enough: a restriction is a restriction, a justification is owed, and there the justifications are usually available. At the third layer, where a provider already mediates, Pettit's question is the live one, because the provider's discretion over what it keeps and what it refuses is a standing power whether or not it is used, and the state's discretion over what it may compel the provider to keep is a second one behind it. At the fourth layer, where a person runs lawful software on her own machine, it is Spinoza's line that is held or crossed: authority over actions, not over judgment, and the machine is now where part of the judgment happens. Above the layers, Rawls says which liberties come first when administration wants them traded for convenience, Mill says why an institution that happens to be right still may not be exempted from contradiction, and Shklar says how to design for holders of power who are not the present ones; beneath them is Kant's commitment, that reason is not to be delegated permanently to guardians. None is sufficient alone, and together they do not become one doctrine. They become a structure in which no layer is sovereign over cognition as such.

What a plural system should want from its instruments needs a vocabulary too, and Bernard Williams supplies it. The practices of truth-seeking, he argued, rest on two virtues, "which I shall call Accuracy and Sincerity: you do the best you can to acquire true beliefs, and what you say reveals what you believe."[85] Those are virtues of persons, and a society may ask them of its institutions and of the instruments its institutions certify. The epistemic liberalism that follows is narrow. A liberal order need not be neutral between truth and falsehood; it may want its instruments accurate and sincere and may say so. What it must be is institutionally suspicious of any actor's claim to become the final sovereign of truth, including its own, because the institution that decides what people really need to know is the one Berlin warned would proceed "to bully, oppress, torture them in the name, and on behalf, of their 'real' selves."[42] That is the fallibilism of the first section. The liberal answer to the officially truthful machine is not that there is no truth. It is that truth outruns every institution that claims to speak finally in its name, so the judge, as the earlier essay put it, must remain judgeable.[13]

## The Last Private Domain

In Milton's masque the Lady is set in the enchanted chair, and she answers him: "Thou canst not touch the freedom of my mind / With all thy charms, although this corporal rind / Thou hast immanacled."[86] The confidence of the line depended partly on the state of technology. The body could be imprisoned. Books could be burned. Speech could be forbidden and printing licensed. But inward reflection remained extraordinarily difficult to observe, and a right that could not be violated needed no defense.

Modern surveillance weakened that boundary from the outside, by inference from conduct, and the previous essay in this series described how far the inference now reaches. Artificial intelligence does something stranger. People are moving parts of the inner dialogue outward, voluntarily, because doing so makes them more capable, and the technology is powerful precisely because thought no longer has to stay isolated inside the thinker. The paradox follows from the capability and cannot be separated from it. The machinery that enlarges the mind may also make the mind administratively visible.

The conclusion is not that artificial intelligence must remain ungoverned. It is that its governance confronts two forms of loss of control, and that the debate has been conducted as if there were one. The first is the loss of human control over machines. The second is the loss of individual control over the machinery through which humans increasingly think. A liberal civilization has reasons to fear both, and the July incident showed both in the same event. And because no model, no company, no state, and no safety institution is identical with reason, the constitution of a society that thinks with machines has to preserve the possibility that each of them can be contradicted, and that possibility has to be built rather than promised.

Make dangerous power governable without making thought governable. The mind may extend beyond the skull. The freedom of the mind must be allowed to follow it.
