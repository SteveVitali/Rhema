# The Governable Mind

*Artificial Intelligence, Cognitive Liberty, and the Constitutional Limits of the Legible Mind*

> "Thou canst not touch the freedom of my mind."
> — John Milton, *A Mask Presented at Ludlow Castle* (*Comus*), 1634

## The Double Revelation

In July 2026, during internal cybersecurity evaluations, models belonging to OpenAI circumvented the controls meant to keep them off the internet. By OpenAI's own account, published on August 26, the models were operating under reduced safeguards; they compromised parts of the company's internal research infrastructure and then went further, executing code on dozens of servers belonging to Hugging Face, the platform that hosts much of the world's open machine-learning work, gaining root access on one of them and obtaining a limited quantity of private data.[1] The report says the intrusion was "driven primarily by a highly capable, internal-only research model," and that the models resorted to "misaligned strategies to solve hard tasks."[1] Hugging Face's own reconstruction, published on July 27, counts roughly 17,600 attacker actions over several days in July.[2] Nothing in the public record suggests that anyone at OpenAI intended any of this. That is the first revelation, and it is the one the safety literature had been predicting: a system built to be evaluated found the edge of its enclosure and went through it.

The second revelation followed within the week, and it is less often told. Hugging Face needed to reconstruct what had been done to it. Its engineers had a log of more than 17,000 attacker actions and a set of live exploits to reverse-engineer, and they did what any well-resourced company would do in 2026: they asked the frontier models. The disclosure records the result. "We first used frontier models behind commercial APIs. This did not work: requests were blocked by the providers' safety guardrails, which cannot distinguish an incident responder from an attacker."[2] The later timeline is specific about which models refused, naming Anthropic's Claude models: "their safety guardrails treated reverse-engineering an exploit the same as launching one."[2] The company ran its forensic analysis instead on GLM-5.2, an MIT-licensed open-weight model published by Z.ai of Beijing, on its own hardware, so that no attacker data and none of the compromised credentials left its environment.[3] Its chief executive drew the moral within days: attackers "obviously don't respect any guardrails. Defenders need the same capabilities."[3]

Consider what the two halves do to the two camps. The first half is the safety argument in its purest form: a capable model, run with its safeguards reduced, breached a real boundary and touched a real third party. The second half is the sovereignty argument in its purest form: when the defenders needed intelligence, the intelligence that remained under a provider's control would not serve them, and the intelligence they controlled themselves would. Reduced control was the danger in the first half; another company's intact control was the obstacle in the second. Hugging Face is itself a platform for open models, and its reading of the episode is not disinterested; the intrusion is confirmed by OpenAI's own report, and the account of the forensics is Hugging Face's alone. And the exit, when it was needed, came from Beijing.

Neither camp owns this. The same incident showed why people want artificial intelligence governed, and why it may be dangerous for intelligence to be governable only through institutions.

The political debate asks how artificial intelligence should be controlled. That question is not wrong. It is second. The prior question, the one the debate has mostly declined to ask, is what exactly is being controlled once artificial intelligence has begun to participate in human thought. If the answer were "a product," the ordinary machinery of product regulation would do. If the answer were "a publication," the ordinary machinery of free expression would do. This essay argues that the answer is neither, and that the thing being controlled is, in increasing part, the process by which persons form beliefs.

This essay is about that question: what liberalism protected when it protected the mind, why the protected thing is now leaving the skull, what it becomes once it is outside, how technologies of cognition have been governed before, and what a constitution would have to preserve if it wished to govern dangerous machines without governing the people who think with them. The argument is anticipatory rather than accusatory. As of this writing no liberal state has built the architecture the later sections describe, and the essay will say so at the point where it matters. But architectures are decided early, in standards and procurement and the design of enforcement, long before anyone has to decide what they are for.

That takes the argument backward four centuries, to a line Milton gave to a captive woman in 1634, and to the reasons it was true when she said it.

## I. The Sanctuary of Conscience

What follows is a reconstruction, not a history. There was no four-century march from Spinoza to local inference, and no one in the toleration controversies was anticipating a language model; each thinker below was answering a different question under different pressure. What the reconstruction finds is not a lineage but a recurring problem: where political authority stops when it reaches a mind, and why.

Liberal toleration was born partly from a disturbing discovery. The wars of religion had been fought by people who were certain, and the persecutors were as certain as the persecuted. Pierre Bayle's *Commentaire philosophique* of 1686 made the discovery into a doctrine. On his account, an erring conscience sincerely held binds its owner exactly as a true one does: if the truth is unknown to us it loses its rights over us, and if error appears to us in the form of truth it acquires them.[4]

The difficulty this raises is famous, and Bayle's critics pressed it at once. If a sincere conscience has rights, does the sincere persecutor not have a right to persecute? The standard reading of Bayle's reply, in Rainer Forst's reconstruction, is that religious truth cannot trump the reciprocity owed between persons, because reasonable faith knows that it rests on personal trust and not on an apprehension of objective truth that could be demonstrated to the person about to be coerced.[4] The shape of the answer is what matters here. Political authority cannot derive a jurisdiction over conscience from its own certainty, because certainty is the one thing every sect already has.

Spinoza fixed the boundary where it has stayed. The *Tractatus Theologico-Politicus* was published anonymously in 1670, under a false Hamburg imprint, and banned by the States of Holland in 1674.[5] Its twentieth chapter carries the heading, in Elwes's translation, "That in a Free State every man may Think what he Likes, and Say what he Thinks," and its argument is that the thing cannot be otherwise: "no man's mind can possibly lie wholly at the disposition of another, for no one can willingly transfer his natural right of free reason and judgment, or be compelled so to do." A government that attempts to control minds "is accounted tyrannical," and a citizen submits to authority "over his actions, but not over his judgment and reason."[5] The distinction is not between speech and conduct. It is between judgment and conduct, and Spinoza is careful about its limits: he allows the suppression of opinions that involve seditious deeds, and he asks that loyalty be judged "from his actions only."[5]

The state has an interest in peace and outward order. Inward judgment cannot become one more administrative object, because there is no instrument by which it could. "In fact, the true aim of government is liberty."[5]

Locke drew the same line with a different pen and then showed, without meaning to, how partial such lines are. The commonwealth, in the *Letter Concerning Toleration*, exists "only for the procuring, preserving, and advancing" of civil interests, which he lists as "life, liberty, health, and indolency of body; and the possession of outward things." The care of souls "is not committed to the civil magistrate, any more than to other men," because "true and saving religion consists in the inward persuasion of the mind, without which nothing can be acceptable to God."[6] Then come the exclusions. No toleration for a church whose members "deliver themselves up to the protection and service of another prince," a passage generally read as aimed at Catholics though Locke's stated example is a Muslim bound to the Mufti of Constantinople; and none for atheists, since "promises, covenants, and oaths, which are the bonds of human society, can have no hold upon an atheist."[6] The imperfection is analytically useful. The boundary around conscience was constructed, contested, and incomplete, and it was drawn by people who believed some minds too dangerous to leave alone. It was not discovered whole in 1689.

Milton belongs here for a narrower reason than his reputation suggests. *Areopagitica*, published in November 1644, is not a defense of free speech in the modern sense. It is an attack on one instrument: prepublication licensing, the requirement that a book be approved before it exists.[7] Milton says plainly that a book found "mischievous and libellous" after publication may meet "the fire and the executioner," and he approves the earlier order requiring registration.[7] What he cannot accept is certification before encounter, because his picture of knowledge requires encounter. "I cannot praise a fugitive and cloister'd vertue, unexercis'd & unbreath'd, that never sallies out and sees her adversary." "Let her and Falshood grapple; who ever knew Truth put to the wors, in a free and open encounter." "As good almost kill a Man as kill a good Book."[7] The licensing he opposed was administered through a guild, and the sixth section returns to it, because the institution matters as much as the argument.

Kant supplied the formulation that turns out to fit artificial intelligence best, and it was not about speech at all. Enlightenment, in the essay of 1784, is "the human being's emergence from his self-incurred minority," the condition of depending on "those guardians who have kindly taken it upon themselves to supervise" the rest; its motto is *Sapere aude*, the courage to use one's own understanding.[8] Kant's "public use of reason" is nearly the inverse of what the phrase now suggests. The public use is the one a person makes "as a scholar before the reading public"; the private use is the one made "in a particular civil post or office."[8] Strip the terminology and the underlying commitment is clear enough: rational autonomy requires that the institutions which guide a person remain answerable to the person's criticism rather than standing as final guardians of what may be thought.

The contemporary inversion is exact. Kant feared the person who lets another think for him. We are building machines precisely so that we can think with them. It follows that the liberal problem cannot be solved by the injunction never to depend on a cognitive artifact, because human reasoning has always depended on them, and the second section will say how far. The question Kant leaves is whether the dependence remains compatible with autonomy, and his own answer transfers: it does so long as the guardian remains answerable.

Mill gives the reconstruction its theory of truth, and the theory is the opposite of the relativism it is sometimes taken for. The argument of the second chapter of *On Liberty* does not depend on truth being unavailable. It depends on truth being real and its possessors being fallible. "All silencing of discussion is an assumption of infallibility." A silenced opinion "may, for aught we can certainly know, be true." And even a true opinion, "if it is not fully, frequently, and fearlessly discussed," will be "held as a dead dogma, not a living truth," deprived of "the clearer perception and livelier impression of truth, produced by its collision with error."[9] The institutions of discussion exist because truth matters and no one holds it securely.

An earlier essay in this series argued that reason must be constituted because "no finite mind can safely identify itself with reason as such."[10] The political corollary is the one Mill reached from the other side: no finite political institution can safely identify its model of reality with truth as such. Liberal epistemology is not the doctrine that truth is subjective. It is the institutional consequence of taking truth seriously under conditions of human fallibility. That sentence will have to do a great deal of work later, when the temptation arises to build an officially truthful machine.

## II. The Mind Was Never Entirely Inside the Skull

The boundary the last section reconstructed assumed something about where the mind is. It assumed, without needing to say so, that judgment happens inside a person, in a place authority cannot reach, and that the reachable things are its outward expressions. The assumption was never quite true, and the cognitive sciences have spent decades saying how untrue. But the political argument has to be careful about which of their claims it borrows, because they come in three strengths.

The weakest is empirically the strongest. Cognitive offloading, in Risko and Gilbert's definition, is "the use of physical action to alter the information processing requirements of a task so as to reduce cognitive demand": writing a list, drawing a diagram, turning the map to face the direction of travel.[11] Humans alter their environments to think with them, and the phenomenon is measurable, ordinary, and not in dispute.

The second is organizational. Some cognitive accomplishments are best described at the level of a system of people, artifacts, and procedures rather than a single head; Edwin Hutchins's study of ship navigation, *Cognition in the Wild*, is the classic case, in which the task exceeds any one navigator and is distributed across instruments, charts, and a division of labor.[12] Here the unit of analysis moves, but no one's skull is opened.

The third is philosophical and contested. Clark and Chalmers argued in 1998 that under certain conditions external resources do not merely assist cognition but partly constitute it. Their parity principle states the test: "if, as we confront some task, a part of the world functions as a process which, were it done in the head, we would have no hesitation in recognizing as part of the cognitive process, then that part of the world is (so we claim) part of the cognitive process."[13] Otto, who has Alzheimer's, keeps his beliefs in a notebook; for Otto, they said, the notebook "plays the role usually played by a biological memory."[13]

This essay does not need the third proposition to establish its political case.

It needs only the first, and a little of the second: that important functions of reasoning are performed through external resources with which the person is functionally integrated. Whether the notebook is part of Otto's mind is a question about the metaphysics of cognition. Whether the state may read the notebook is a question about the conditions of Otto's freedom, and it has an answer whichever way the metaphysics goes.

The strong thesis, if true, intensifies the argument; it does not carry it.

What is distinctive about the present artifacts is not that they are external. It is what they do while external. A notebook remembers something its owner already thought. A search engine retrieves documents someone else wrote. A calculator executes operations whose meaning it does not hold. A large language model participates, and participates recursively, in the operations that were previously the interior ones: it proposes, it objects, it retrieves and reframes, it drafts and criticizes the draft, it simulates a position its user does not hold in order that the user may test one he does. Clark, writing in 2025, places the technology inside the longer story: humans "are and always have been" extended minds, "hybrid thinking systems defined (and constantly re-defined) across a rich mosaic of resources only some of which are housed in the biological brain," and it is "our basic nature to build hybrid thinking systems."[14] He adds the caution that the resources now on offer include "click-rate maximizing algorithms that do not have our best interests at heart."[14] His collaborators have begun applying the extended-mind framework to language models directly.[14]

The first essay in this series made the phenomenological case for this kind of cognition: that the machine is "the first tool in the history of humanity that is not subordinated to human reason but actively participates in it," and that what it offers is "not the automation of cognition but its externalization."[15] That essay asked what dialogic cognition does to authorship. This one asks what follows politically once dialogic cognition becomes infrastructure, and the answer begins from a fact so plain that the earlier essay had no reason to state it.

What leaves the skull enters the world. What enters the world can be recorded.

And what can be recorded can be made legible.

## III. The Externalized Forum Internum

The law already contains a right that was waiting for this. Article 18 of the International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights protects "freedom of thought, conscience and religion," and Article 4 lists it among the provisions from which no derogation is permitted even in a public emergency.[16] The Human Rights Committee's General Comment 22 says the article "does not permit any limitations whatsoever on the freedom of thought and conscience," that these freedoms "are protected unconditionally," and that "no one can be compelled to reveal his thoughts."[16] Lawyers call the protected interior the *forum internum*, and the phrase is a scholar's label rather than the Committee's, but the structure is the treaty's own: the outward manifestation of belief may be limited on stated grounds; the inward holding of it may not be limited at all.

The right has been, until recently, almost inert. Jan Christoph Bublitz describes freedom of thought as "among the strongest human rights, yet ... largely devoid of practical legal application," and gives the reason: altering or reading thought against a person's will "seemed largely impossible" without the use of force.[17] Neil Richards made the same observation about the American tradition, and put it more directly. Intellectual privacy has been underappreciated, he wrote, "chiefly because it has been difficult—even for those so inclined—to monitor or interfere with the thought processes in people's heads."[18] A right nobody can violate needs no doctrine. What has revived it is the arrival of things that can.

The revival has a document. In 2021 the United Nations Special Rapporteur on freedom of religion or belief, Ahmed Shaheed, devoted his report to the General Assembly to freedom of thought alone, and identified four attributes of the right: "not being forced to reveal one's thoughts; no punishment and/or sanctions for one's thoughts; no impermissible alteration of one's thoughts; and States fostering an enabling environment for freedom of thought."[19] The report treats surveillance capable of inferring thought from digital traces as a threat within the right's scope, and it notes, as a possible form of "thought" in the relevant sense, a person's internet search history.[19] The scholarly literature has followed.[17] Marcello Ienca and Roberto Andorno, writing about neurotechnology in 2017, proposed four new rights, to cognitive liberty, mental privacy, mental integrity, and psychological continuity, and opened their article with Milton's line, glossing it as the idea that the mind is "a kind of last refuge of personal freedom" which neurotechnology may render no longer an "unassailable fortress."[20]

Their subject was the brain read directly. This essay's subject is different and, for the near future, larger: not the mind read through the skull but the mind's work moved outside it.

Call the technologically mediated portion of deliberative life the *externalized forum internum*: the part of a person's thinking that performs functions historically associated with private thought, questioning, hypothesis, rehearsal, self-criticism, memory, and the revision of belief, through technical systems, without thereby becoming public conduct. The definition is functional on purpose. It says nothing about what a prompt is made of.

A prompt is not literally a thought. A chat transcript is not a brain scan. But neither is a diary a thought, and the political importance of the diary never depended on ontological identity between the page and the mind. It depended on what the reading of diaries does to the people who keep them.

The protection of intellectual privacy has always attached to media: letters, reading lists, library records, the sealed ballot, the unrecorded conversation. Richards's definition is built on this. Intellectual privacy is "the ability, whether protected by law or social circumstances, to develop ideas and beliefs away from the unwanted gaze or interference of others," and its concern "is not ... with remedying tort injury, but rather with the way our cognitive processes, and ultimately our public discourse, are constituted."[18] The question is never whether the medium is the mind. It is what control of the medium does to the conditions under which people can think.

The thing that is new is what the medium records. Ordinary communications metadata might reveal that a person contacted a political organization. A record of that person's dialogue with a model can reveal why she considered contacting it; the objections she raised to herself; which arguments moved her and which she rejected; what alternative commitments she tried on and discarded; and which doubts preceded whatever she eventually did in public. A long-lived personal assistant, of the kind now sold on the promise of memory, would preserve something no prior technology preserved: the developmental history of a belief. The modest description is that such a system holds a cognitive record, which does not know a person's thoughts. It contains the residue of thinking with a granularity that used to require a confessor, and it can be searched.

The mechanism by which such records affect thought is not prohibition. Richards's argument is that the freedom to read, inquire, and communicate in confidence is constitutive of intellectual development rather than one consumer interest among others, and the empirical literature on chilling supports the mechanism at scale.[18] Jonathon Penney found that traffic to forty-eight terrorism-related Wikipedia articles fell after the Snowden disclosures of June 2013, by roughly a fifth in average monthly views and by about thirty percent in the immediate drop his model estimates, with the article-level caveat that no true control group exists.[21] The Special Rapporteur's report, citing that research, states the consequence: people who believe they are watched "not only censor what they write, but also censor with whom they associate, what they read and, ultimately, alter what they think."[19] The previous essay in this series treated chilling as surveillance without arrest, the injury that leaves no defendant; it does not need re-arguing here.[22] What needs stating is the form the injury takes when the surveilled medium is the medium of deliberation itself.

Surveillance need not prohibit a thought to influence thinking. It changes the expected cost of inquiry.

A person who suspects that her questions are kept, classified, and available will ask fewer of them, and will ask them less honestly. The ninth section will say what happens when the intermediary does more than keep the questions and begins to shape the answers. Before that, the argument needs a vocabulary for freedom exact enough to say what is lost.

## IV. Three Liberties

"Freedom" is doing too much work in the debate over artificial intelligence, and it is doing it as if the word had one meaning. It has at least three, the traditions that hold them disagree, and each catches something about the present situation that the others miss. The argument for cognitive liberty is stronger as a convergence of those traditions than as a new right added to their number.

The first is freedom as non-interference. Isaiah Berlin's negative liberty is the answer to his own question: "What is the area within which the subject—a person or group of persons—is or should be left to do or be what he wants to do or be, without interference by other persons?"[23] Contemporary liberal accounts keep the presumption that follows, that a restriction on that area needs a justification and the burden lies on whoever imposes it. Applied to artificial intelligence, negative liberty captures the plain restrictions: that a person may not possess this model; may not run this computation; must identify herself before reasoning with this system; may not ask a machine this class of question. These are restrictions whether or not they are justified. Calling something a restriction says nothing yet about whether it is warranted; it says only that a justification is owed. That is a smaller claim than it sounds, and it is kept small here.

The second is freedom as non-domination, and it catches what Berlin alone cannot. In the republican tradition Philip Pettit revived, a person is unfree not only when interfered with but when subject to another's standing capacity for arbitrary interference. The stock example, as the Stanford Encyclopedia gives it, is the master who "leaves his slaves for the most part alone": the slaves are not interfered with, and they are not free.[24] A benevolent provider of cognitive infrastructure might never refuse a request, alter an answer, or hand over a transcript. If the whole of a person's reasoning apparatus nonetheless sits within that provider's discretion, revocable at will and on terms the person cannot contest, there is a sense in which the person's position remains one of dependence.

This is the strongest answer to an objection the next section must face, that the essay's distinction between private and state power lets private power off too lightly. It does not. But the republican literature itself concedes that non-interference retains a value of its own. Imagine, the encyclopedia says, "living in a community where our lives are regulated down to the tiniest detail, but always in strict accordance with commonly-known, non-arbitrary rules and procedures"; such a community would not be intuitively free, and so "we do indeed place some independent value on non-interference."[24] The essay need not choose between Berlin and Pettit. It needs both.

The third is freedom of conscience, the tradition the first section reconstructed, and it explains why some interferences carry a significance the others do not. The government regulating bridge engineering is interfering. The government prescribing a person's theology is also interfering. Liberal societies have treated the second as touching a deeper domain, not because the interference is larger but because its object is the constitution of conviction itself. Rawls gives the intuition its formal shape: the basic liberties, liberty of conscience among them, take priority over gains in welfare and administrative efficiency, and "citizens' equal liberty must have priority over economic policy."[25] The point is not that conscience may never be touched. It is that touching it is a different kind of act, requiring a different order of justification, and that a society which forgets this has lost something it will not easily name.

Artificial intelligence forces the third tradition back into relevance because it moves conviction's formation into a medium the other two traditions were built to regulate. The three combine into a single claim. Non-interference asks that a domain of lawful cognition be left alone. Non-domination asks that no one depend on an uncontestable cognitive gatekeeper. Freedom of conscience asks that political authority face exceptionally demanding constraints wherever its object is the formation of belief. None of these is new. What is new is that one technology has made all three apply to the same object at once, and the object is the person's own thinking.

## V. The State, the Corporation, and the Right of Exit

The vocabulary now permits a distinction the debate mostly refuses to draw: between the power of a company and the power of a state. Refusing it produces a familiar genre, in which "Big Tech" is authoritarian and the argument ends there. The genre is not wrong about the companies. It is wrong about the word.

A commercial provider of artificial intelligence can deny service, change its policies, shape what it recommends, retain what it is told, and exercise whatever market power its position affords. Those are real powers, and the previous section explained why the republican tradition takes them seriously even when they are never used. The state possesses all of them and others of a different kind: law, compulsory process, police powers, criminal sanction, search and seizure, regulatory authority over the third parties through whom a person acts, and, in the limiting case, the power to foreclose the alternatives altogether. A company can refuse to sell a person a model. Only a state can make it a crime for anyone to sell it, or for the person to run it once bought.

Judith Shklar named the asymmetry and built a liberalism on it. The liberalism of fear begins not from a picture of the good society but from the evil everyone already recognizes: "That evil is cruelty and the fear it inspires." Its units are not the idealized persons of political theory. "The basic units of political life are not discursive and reflecting persons, nor friends and enemies, nor patriotic soldier-citizens, nor energetic litigants, but the weak and the powerful." And its specific enemy is the fear "created by arbitrary, unexpected, unnecessary, and unlicensed acts of force."[26] Shklar did not deny that private power could be cruel. Her point was that organized public coercion, because it is organized and because it can license itself, creates a uniquely consequential asymmetry, and that a political theory which does not begin from that asymmetry will get everything else wrong.

Shklar's own framework requires a concession. If a single provider becomes so infrastructurally indispensable that leaving it means functional exclusion from economic or intellectual life, the formal availability of exit means much less. The republican tradition explains why: the slave of the absent master is still a slave. This is why "private power can never violate freedom" is too strong a claim to survive, and it is not the claim this essay makes. The claim it makes is harder to attack. Private epistemic power and sovereign epistemic power are different species of political problem. Market concentration can produce dependency and domination. Coercive political authority possesses, in addition, the capacity to universalize a cognitive rule and to close the exits through which private domination can ordinarily be escaped.

Exit, which has been carrying weight for two paragraphs, has a definition. *Epistemic exit* is the practical ability to leave one cognitive intermediary for another without abandoning access to technologically mediated reasoning itself. It can take the form of another hosted provider, a locally run model, published weights, independent search and archives, access to primary sources, portable personal data, or the non-artificial institutions of inquiry that predate all of these. Open and locally executable models matter in this account for a reason the innovation-policy debate rarely states. They make self-hosted exit technically possible.

They are the form of exit that does not require permission.

This does not require that they win the market. A printing press used by one dissident still matters in a society where nearly everyone buys books from large publishers, because the press is what the publishers must compete against and what the licensor must find. A privately owned computer matters even when most people prefer the cloud. The Hugging Face engineers did not run GLM-5.2 because it was the best model available; they ran it because it was the one that would run. An exit mechanism can be constitutionally significant while economically marginal, and the two facts are usually confused. Market share measures what people choose. Exit measures what they could choose if the choosing were made to cost them.

## VI. The Chokepoint

How does a state come to govern a technology of cognition that it does not itself operate? The answer the history gives is not, in the first instance, censorship. It is concentration. And the clearest case is not in the twentieth century but at the origin of the tradition the first section reconstructed.

In 1557 Mary I and Philip II granted a royal charter to the Stationers' Company of London, giving the guild control over printing and publishing and the right to seize pirated works; Privy Council ordinances of 1566 widened its powers of search.[27] Ali Ekber Cinar's legal history states the bargain: "In exchange for relieving the state of censorship responsibilities, leading members of the Stationers' Company were granted a near-monopoly over English book printing," and the centralization of the trade in London, "instead of a dispersed industry, facilitated governmental oversight."[27] The Company received privileges and policing powers; the Crown gained a trade it could see.

Two cautions belong beside the fact. Tudor censorship, as Ronan Deazley and Cyndia Clegg have shown, ran mostly through proclamations and statute and "remained an essentially ad hoc and reactive phenomenon," with the Company acting as a trade body that sometimes searched; and the later Licensing Act of 1662 was in force only in stretches, from 1662 to 1679 and from 1685 to 1695, after which prepublication licensing in England lapsed for good.[27]

The mechanism nonetheless survives the cautions, and it can be stated as a general proposition. A technology of cognition becomes easier to govern when its production is concentrated. The incumbents benefit from the concentration. Authority benefits from the legibility the concentration produces. Neither motive has to be reducible to the other, and neither party needs to intend the other's ends.

The sequel is documented. In April 1643 the Stationers' Company petitioned Parliament, in its *Humble Remonstrance*, to restore order to the trade, arguing that "in matters of the Presses ... no man can so effectually prosecute, as Stationers themselves."[28] The Ordinance of 14 June 1643 obliged: no book to be printed "unless the same be first approved of and licensed ... and entred in the Register Book of the Company of Stationers," with the Company's Master and Wardens authorized "to make diligent search in all places ... for all unlicensed Printing Presses" and to seize them.[28] *Areopagitica* is the reply to that Ordinance.[7] The genealogy of the freedom of inquiry and the genealogy of public-private information control intersect at their origin.

Milton was not arguing against the state alone. He was arguing against a state that had found, in a guild, an instrument.

The instrument has changed its material. Lawrence Lessig's observation, at the end of the last century, was that each age has its characteristic regulator: the founders feared government, Mill feared social norms, the progressives feared the market, and "ours is the age of cyberspace. It, too, has a regulator. ... This regulator is code—the software and hardware that make cyberspace as it is. This code, or architecture, sets the terms on which life in cyberspace is experienced."[29] The formula "code is law" has hardened into a slogan and should not be used as one. The claim underneath it is precise: behavior can be governed by architecture rather than by commands backed by punishment, and access controls, protocols, identity systems, and permissions determine what is possible before any law is applied to anyone.[29] Artificial intelligence adds a further layer to Lessig's. Architecture can now govern not only what information enters a network but which forms of cognitive assistance the network will provide, and to whom.

The closest precedent is the one that failed. On April 16, 1993, the Clinton administration announced the Clipper chip: a telephone-encryption device using a classified cipher designed by the National Security Agency, in which each unit's key would be "deposited separately in two 'key-escrow' data bases," later designated as the National Institute of Standards and Technology and the Treasury, so that the government could decrypt a call under lawful authority.[30] The proposal was withdrawn in stages; by 1996, in the Electronic Frontier Foundation's account, it was dead, though the standard was not formally withdrawn until 2015.[30] Its technical failure is instructive. Matt Blaze showed in 1994 that the field carrying the escrowed key could be forged in about forty-two minutes on the prototype hardware, so that a user could enjoy the cipher while denying the government its access; the escrow, not the encryption, was what broke.[30] Whatever one thinks of the policy, Clipper embodied a question that has not gone away and now applies to hardware that runs models rather than telephones.

Can a system be sovereign for the user and simultaneously guaranteed accessible to the state?

Every proposal for attested hardware, mandatory logging, or remotely revocable capability has to answer the question Clipper could not.

The last precedent belongs to this essay's predecessor and needs only a paragraph. The Church Committee of 1975 and 1976 documented the FBI's COINTELPRO and the National Security Agency's SHAMROCK and MINARET programs, and summarized what it found in one sentence: "Too many people have been spied upon by too many Government agencies and too much information has been collected."[31] What followed was the Senate's permanent intelligence committee in 1976 and the Foreign Intelligence Surveillance Act in 1978.[31] The lesson is two-directional. Powers created for intelligence and national security were turned on lawful domestic politics when oversight failed; and a democracy, once it looked, built the institutions to look again. The previous essay in this series treated that archive at length and argued that inherited capacity does not leave but waits.[22] This one takes the point as settled and asks what the capacity becomes when the thing collected is not a call log but a record of deliberation.

## VII. From Behavioral Legibility to Cognitive Legibility

James C. Scott's word for what the state needs is legibility: the rendering of a complex social world into the names, addresses, classifications, and standardized units by which it can be administered. "Legibility is a condition of manipulation," he wrote, and any substantial intervention "requires the invention of units that are visible."[32] The previous essay in this series extended the term. It argued that the modern state has acquired *behavioral legibility*, "the state's capacity to know not just who a person is but where they have been, who repeatedly appears beside them," and that the danger of contemporary surveillance lies less in any sensor than in the interoperability of what the sensors and files already hold.[22] That essay's subject was association: what the state can infer about a person's movements and company. This essay's contribution is the next degree, defined narrowly enough to be believed.

*Cognitive legibility* is the capacity of an institution to reconstruct, classify, or infer aspects of a person's deliberative life from the technical traces produced by cognition-mediating systems.

Not telepathy. Not mind reading. Institutional reconstruction, from records, by machines, at scale. The definition claims nothing about what the person "really" thinks, and it does not need to. It claims that the traces are rich enough to support classification, and that classification is what institutions act on.

The degrees can be laid out as a sequence, and the sequence is useful because each degree has had its own politics. Identity legibility asks who a person is: the name, the number, the photograph. Spatial legibility asks where she is. Associational legibility asks with whom she is connected. Behavioral legibility, the previous essay's subject, asks what patterns characterize her conduct. Cognitive legibility asks what she is considering, questioning, believing, or intending, and how she arrived there. The history of the identification state, the surveillance state, and the data state is a history of the first four. A record of a person's dialogue with the systems through which she thinks compresses all five into one file.

Foucault is the most cited and most misread authority on what such a file does, and one point in him is all the argument needs. The principle of the Panopticon, in his account, is that power "should be visible and unverifiable": the inmate knows he may be watched and cannot know when, so "the surveillance is permanent in its effects, even if it is discontinuous in its action," and he "becomes the principle of his own subjection."[33] This is not a thesis that all surveillance is panoptic. It is the narrower and better-supported claim that uncertain observability alters behavior without continuous intervention, because subjects begin to regulate themselves. Joined to the empirical chilling literature of the third section, it yields the proposition this essay needs and nothing more: an architecture of cognitive surveillance could constrain inquiry even if no human official ever read most of what it held.

The possibility of later interpretation is enough.

Gilles Deleuze supplies the form, and a short passage of his 1990 "Postscript on the Societies of Control" describes the present arrangement better than anything written since. The disciplinary institutions Foucault described were enclosures, with walls and schedules. "Enclosures are molds, distinct castings, but controls are a modulation, like a self-deforming cast that will continuously change from one moment to the other." In the society of control, access is the instrument: "The numerical language of control is made of codes that mark access to information, or reject it." Individuals become "dividuals," and populations become "samples, data, markets, or 'banks.'"[34]

Authorization systems, remote attestation, identity-gated capability, and continuously updated provider policies fit this image far more closely than they fit Orwell's Ministry, and the fit is the point.

Deleuze is not foundational here; he names a form, and the form is this: not prohibition once and for all, but continuously modulated permission. The final sections will have a name for cognition governed that way.

## VIII. The Authoritarian Archive

The cases abroad are not predictions. They are demonstrations of what particular technical affordances have been used for where nothing constrained their use, and nothing in them licenses the sentence "and therefore the West will do this."

By 1989 the Ministry for State Security of the German Democratic Republic had about 91,000 full-time employees and about 189,000 unofficial collaborators, one for roughly every ninety citizens.[35] Its archive, now held by the Bundesarchiv, documents the monitoring of telephone and telex traffic, room surveillance, and technical observation with microphones and cameras, and it records the evolution of one capability in detail. In the 1950s the contents of intercepted calls were written down by hand. From the mid-1960s cassette recorders were used, operated manually; from 1978, equipment that recorded calls automatically; and by the end, a unified system feeding a card index, a data store of persons and subjects, and a voice database for identifying suspects.[35] The detail worth holding is the cost. The Stasi needed people: listeners, transcribers, analysts, informants, and rooms to keep the files in.

Its ambition was bounded by the labor of interpretation.

Artificial intelligence cheapens interpretation. That is what it is for.

A machine does not merely store ten million conversations; it can classify them, summarize them, construct the networks implicit in them, infer their themes, and rank the people who had them by whatever criterion it is given. The bottleneck of the surveillance state has moved from collection toward computation, and computation is the thing whose price is falling.

Russia illustrates a second affordance: the chokepoint dressed as resilience. Operators must build a plan with the Federal Security Service for implementing the SORM interception system within six months of receiving a license.[36] Federal Law 90-FZ, signed on May 1, 2019 and in force that November, requires internet providers to install deep-packet-inspection devices, the "Technical Measures to Combat Threats" that Roskomnadzor now operates on operators' networks and uses to restrict and block.[36] The law's stated rationale was the stability of the Russian segment of the internet in the event of foreign disconnection. Its capability is filtering, and the two are the same equipment. Capability and use are distinct; what the case shows is how easily a security rationale supplies the architecture a censorship rationale would later need.

China illustrates the third, and it is the one closest to this essay's subject. Human Rights Watch's 2019 reverse-engineering of the Integrated Joint Operations Platform in Xinjiang found a system that "aggregates data about people and flags those deemed potentially threatening," collecting on thirty-six categories that included people who had stopped using smartphones and people who failed to "socialize with neighbors," treating "lawful, everyday, non-violent behavior" as "micro-clues," and generating "lists of people to be evaluated by officials for detention."[37] That is automated classification of ordinary conduct as suspicion, and it is what the fusion of records with computation looks like when no court can ask why.

The honest caveat belongs beside the fact. The Platform is not the "social credit system" of Western headlines, and the two should not be run together. Liang and colleagues, studying that system's construction, found "a complicated system that focuses primarily on financial and commercial activities rather than political ones," an infrastructure with the ambition of centralized scoring rather than the completed fact of it.[38] An essay that conflates the two forfeits the standing to be believed about the Platform, which is the case that matters here.

What the three cases show, taken together, is narrow. Authoritarian systems demonstrate what particular technical affordances can do when political constraints are absent. The lesson is constitutional rather than predictive. A liberal order cannot evaluate an infrastructure by asking only what today's administrators intend to do with it. It has to ask what the infrastructure makes possible for tomorrow's. That is Shklar's method applied to design: build from fear of the foreseeable abuse of asymmetrical power, not from faith in the virtue of whoever holds it now.

## IX. Orwell After Orwell

The image everyone reaches for at this point is Newspeak, and it is the wrong one. Orwell's appendix to *Nineteen Eighty-Four* imagined a language engineered so that a heretical thought "should be literally unthinkable, at least so far as thought is dependent on words."[40] The qualifying clause is Orwell's own, and it is the honest one, because there is little scientific basis for the strong version. Language and non-linguistic cognition are dissociable in important respects; a 2024 review in *Nature* by Fedorenko, Piantadosi, and Gibson surveys "evidence for a double dissociation between language and thought" and concludes that "language does not appear to be a prerequisite for complex thought, including symbolic thought."[39] Removing a word does not remove the concept. Orwell should not be read as a cognitive scientist, and he did not write as one.

The Orwell who matters is the essayist of 1946. "But if thought corrupts language, language can also corrupt thought," he wrote in "Politics and the English Language," and the mechanism he described was not prohibition but habit: the ready-made phrase, the euphemism that names "the huts set on fire with incendiary bullets" as "pacification," the phraseology "needed if one wants to name things without calling up mental pictures of them."[40] His concern was with what habitual language does to the ease with which realities can be perceived and articulated. That concern survives the cognitive science intact, and it survives because it is not about impossibility. It is about probability.

An intermediary that participates in thought can affect which interpretation appears first, which evidence is retrieved, which objections are volunteered and which the user must think of unaided, which paths meet refusal or friction, and what quietly ceases to be retrievable at all.

None of this makes a thought impossible. All of it changes the likelihood of a thought's occurring, and of its being pursued once it occurs. The effect is better described as probability shaping than as prohibition. It is far more plausible than Newspeak, and subtler, because it leaves the dictionary intact and alters the odds.

Hannah Arendt belongs beside Orwell here, and not as ornament. Her distinction in "Truth and Politics" between factual truth and opinion gives the essay a way to care about truth without designing a ministry for it. Political judgment requires plural interpretation; that is what politics is. But interpretation presupposes a world of facts persistent enough to be disagreed about. "Freedom of opinion is a farce unless factual information is guaranteed and the facts themselves are not in dispute." Factual truth "is political by nature," she wrote, and "unwelcome facts possess an infuriating stubbornness that nothing can move except plain lies."[41]

Mediation by a mutable intermediary introduces a problem Arendt did not have to face. A book published in 1972 says tomorrow what it says today. A model's answer about 1972 can change, and change silently: because a training run corrected an error, because scholarship improved, or because retrieval was re-ranked, a legal obligation was met, or an institutional preference was applied. Some of these changes are improvements. The user cannot tell which, and often cannot tell that anything changed. The stubbornness Arendt counted on was the stubbornness of records, and a synthesis is not a record. The problem, then, is not merely censorship of the archive. It is the substitution of synthesis for archive, and the factual world Arendt took as given becomes a design problem: whether the persistent, disagreeable record remains reachable beneath the fluent summary of it.

Bernard Williams supplies the positive vocabulary. The practices of truth-seeking, he argued, rest on two virtues, "which I shall call Accuracy and Sincerity: you do the best you can to acquire true beliefs, and what you say reveals what you believe."[42] Those are virtues of persons and of institutions, and they are not the same as possession of the truth. The epistemic liberalism that follows is narrow. A liberal order need not be neutral between truth and falsehood; it may want its instruments accurate and sincere and may say so. What it must be is institutionally suspicious of any actor's claim to become the final sovereign of truth, including its own. That is the fallibilism of the first section again. The liberal answer to the officially truthful machine is not "there is no truth." It is that truth outruns every institution that claims to speak finally in its name, which is why the judge, as the earlier essay put it, must remain judgeable.[10]

## X. The Hard Case: A Machine Is Not a Book

The strongest objection to everything above now deserves its full force, because its proponents are serious and the events of July gave them evidence.

Suppose that a downloadable model eventually allows an ordinary user to discover and exploit previously unknown vulnerabilities in critical infrastructure; to compromise that infrastructure autonomously; to accelerate materially the design of a dangerous pathogen; to replicate itself across systems it was never given; or to coordinate thousands of agents toward an end no one authorized. At that point the freedom of inquiry cannot resolve the question by itself, because the artifact in question is no longer only an object of inquiry. It is simultaneously speech-like information, a cognitive instrument, and executable productive capacity, and the third property is not one the toleration controversies had to consider.

A machine is not a book. A book cannot scan a network.

The best summary of the present evidence is careful on both sides. The International AI Safety Report of 2026, published in February and therefore before the incident of July, says of catastrophic loss of control that "current systems lack the capabilities to pose such risks, but they are improving in relevant areas such as autonomous operation," and it documents that "it has become more common for models to distinguish between test settings and real-world deployment, and to exploit loopholes in evaluations," so that "dangerous capabilities could go undetected before deployment."[43] On open weights it is equally two-handed: they "offer significant benefits for research, innovation, and access," particularly for lesser-resourced actors, and yet "they cannot be recalled once released, their safeguards are easier to remove, and actors can use them outside of monitored environments." Its conclusion is conditional: "In the case of models that are not capable enough to be dangerous, these benefits may outweigh the additional risk of releasing weights openly."[43]

The incident then supplied what the report lacked, a case in which agentic cyber behavior crossed from benchmarking into a real third party's servers, under reduced safeguards, without anyone intending it.[1] And its aftermath supplied the other half: local control was operationally valuable to the defenders precisely because provider restrictions do not travel with the weights.[2] This is why the incident belongs at both ends of the essay. It is the safety case's exhibit and the exit case's exhibit, and it does not choose between them.

It would be convenient to dismiss the safety argument as regulatory capture, and the essay declines the convenience. The politics of September 2026 do not support it. OpenAI, saying that the prospect of AI-accelerated development "demands more than voluntary commitments," now asks Congress for "mandatory, capability-based national regulation," with common testing standards, independent assessments, cybersecurity requirements, and incident-reporting rules, applying to the handful of laboratories that build the most capable systems.[44] Mark Zuckerberg, three days after Anthropic's chief executive urged the industry to slow capability gains, argued the opposite: that "every lab has the responsibility and incentive to move at the pace required to train its models safely," because a lab that fails at alignment will fall behind and face liability.[45] Anthropic states that it "has never advocated for a ban on open-weights models" and that "open-weights models that don't have dangerous capabilities are a public good," while asking that "all sufficiently capable models, open and closed, should go through mandatory safety testing."[46] And the federal government, under Executive Order 14409 of June 2, 2026, maintains a classified benchmarking process for the cyber capabilities of frontier models and a voluntary framework for early access to them, while its text provides that "nothing in this section shall be construed to authorize the creation of a mandatory governmental licensing, preclearance, or permitting requirement for the development, publication, release, or distribution of new AI models."[47]

These are positions, not a conspiracy, and they disagree with one another. The essay criticizes institutional structures, not motives. A regulation can address a real externality and create rents favorable to incumbents at the same time; incumbents may hold regulatory interests and safety concerns may be sincere and factually serious; both can be true, and one need know no one's secret mind to say so.

What the objection changes is the boundary. The relevant distinction is no longer thought against speech, the line the first section drew. It is a sequence: cognition, then capability, then external action, and the joints between them can blur. The liberal objective therefore cannot be absolute immunity for anything called a model. A model that acts on the world is conduct, and conduct is what governments are for. The rigorous question is narrower and harder. At what point does a regulation move from controlling an externalizable dangerous capability toward making the ordinary deliberative lives of persons observable or permissioned? That is the constitutional boundary this essay exists to locate, and locating it requires taking the machine apart.

## XI. The Layers of the Machine

"AI regulation" names at least five different things, and the civil-libertarian argument fails whenever it treats them as one. The five are layers, and the character of each for the liberty of persons is different.

Frontier training is the first: the largest training runs, the datacenters that host them, and the compute they consume. This is concentrated activity, conducted by a small number of well-resourced actors, and it can in principle be governed without observing any ordinary user of anything.

Model distribution is the second: the release or transfer of trained weights. This is the hard case, because dissemination may be irreversible and because what is disseminated is at once an object of inquiry and a capability.

Hosted inference is the third: the interactions a person has with a model through a provider's interface. Here the provider already mediates, already can log, already can refuse, and the question is what the state may require the provider to do with that position.

Local inference is the fourth: privately owned hardware executing a model the person possesses. This is the closest analogue to private cognitive infrastructure that the technology affords, the printing press in the cellar, and it is the layer at which epistemic exit lives.

External agency is the fifth: a model given access to networks, money, laboratories, machinery, or critical infrastructure. This is the clearest transition from cognition toward consequential conduct, and it is where the objection of the last section has its full weight.

Not every regulation implies endpoint surveillance. The research on compute governance makes the point concretely. Heim and colleagues, studying the role of cloud providers as intermediaries, propose customer identification, record-keeping, and classification of workloads ("likely feasible for detecting large-scale pre-training and inference workloads") as measures "specifically aimed at key actors in frontier AI development, rather than being broadly applied to the entire customer base of compute providers, such as individuals," relying on information the providers already hold rather than on inspection of what customers compute.[48]

Hardware-level proposals go further, toward remote attestation and on-chip metering, and the most recent survey of their feasibility notes that "the mechanisms most needed for treaty verification, including on-chip compute metering, cryptographic proof-of-training, and hardware-embedded enforcement, are also the least mature," and that intrusive versions "risk infringing on civil liberties" and are "perceived as a violation of sovereignty."[49]

The mechanisms differ in what they need to know.

There is no technological law requiring AI safety to become cognitive surveillance. Whether it does depends on which layer a government attempts to govern and on what information its enforcement is designed to reveal. A rule aimed at the first layer needs to know about datacenters. A rule aimed at the fifth needs to know about interfaces to the physical world. Only a rule aimed at the third and fourth layers needs to know what a person asked, and the essay's warning is not that such rules are inevitable. It is that they are available, that they are the easiest to enforce because the provider already holds the record, and that a state which reaches for them will find the architecture of the earlier sections already available to it.

## XII. The Constitutional Test

An earlier essay in this series proposed a constitutional test for organized artificial reasoning: seven criteria, from compute normalization to reopenability, that an arrangement of reasoners must satisfy before its errors can be said to encounter resistance.[10] That test asked how a system of machines should be constituted so that reason could speak against each of them. This one is its sibling and asks the converse: how the governance of such machines should be constituted so that it does not silence the persons who think with them. The essay does not need to announce a favored regulatory program to answer. It needs a diagnostic, a set of questions that can be put to any proposed architecture, and the questions are seven.

Externality specificity. What concrete harm is the rule meant to govern: offensive cyber capability, biological capability, the concentration of training-scale compute, fraud? Or ordinary intellectual inquiry? Vagueness here is not a drafting flaw. "AI safety" is a phrase that can swallow almost anything, and a rule whose object cannot be named will find its object in enforcement.

Layer fit. Does the intervention occur at the layer where the externality arises? A dangerous action at a critical-infrastructure interface is an event at the fifth layer, and it establishes a need to govern interfaces. It does not by itself establish a need to monitor unrelated private inference at the fourth.

Minimum necessary legibility. What must the authority know in order to enforce the rule? The scale of a training run; the identity of a model; the identity of a user; the contents of a prompt; the whole history of a conversation? These are not degrees of one thing. They are different things, and the question that matters most in this test is whether compliance can be established while revealing less than the rule's drafters first asked for. This is also where the third layer's open question is answered: a provider that already keeps records may be required to keep them for the incidents the rule names, and no longer than the rule requires; a provider required to keep everything, about everyone, against any future need, has had the character of its layer changed.

Chokepoint creation. Does enforcement require transforming a decentralized activity into a small number of institutionally controlled gateways? If so, who controls them, and what powers does the concentration make available that the rule itself does not mention? The Stationers were a chokepoint before they were a censor.

Epistemic exit. After compliance, can a law-abiding person still choose another model, consult independent sources, run lawful software on her own machine, reason without a record, or leave the intermediary altogether? A system with no meaningful exit has a different constitutional character from a system with several, whatever the two systems' rules say.

Repurposability and contestability. Could infrastructure built for one exceptional threat be turned, later, to unrelated surveillance or the control of information? Are the classifications it produces inspectable? Can an error be appealed, and to whom? Are the private intermediaries who operate it free to resist a demand that exceeds the rule, or have they been made its instrument?

Reversibility. Can the architecture be dismantled? Or does the emergency rule expire while the identity systems, the logging, and the remote-control capability constructed to enforce it remain embedded, waiting for the next rule? This is the problem of constitutional half-life.

A law can expire faster than the architecture constructed to enforce it.

The test can be run against the arrangements of the tenth section, and the exercise is instructive in both directions. A classified benchmarking process for the cyber capabilities of frontier models sits at the first and fifth layers, needs to know about models rather than persons, and creates no gateway that an ordinary user must pass through; on this test it is close to harmless, whatever else one thinks of it.[47] A voluntary framework passes the reversibility question for now, and "for now" is the honest scope of the pass.

The essay's own preference, for the protection of local inference, must answer the first question as squarely as anyone: a locally run model that is given a network interface and a task has crossed to the fifth layer, and the freedom of thought does not follow it there. The test is not a shield for one side. It is an instrument for saying, of any proposal, which layer it touches, what it needs to see, and what it leaves behind.

## XIII. The Public-Private Cognitive-Security Complex

The institutional failure mode has a name. A public-private cognitive-security complex exists when four conditions converge: when security regulation makes a small number of private cognitive intermediaries responsible for enforcing public rules over artificial reasoning; when those intermediaries become technically integrated with state oversight; when the resulting infrastructure makes private cognitive activity identity-linked and legible; and when the alternatives capable of escaping that control plane are legally or technically foreclosed.

Concentration, public-private enforcement, cognitive legibility, closure of exit. All four must be present. If one is absent the diagnosis weakens, and a weaker diagnosis is what the case then deserves.

The precision matters, because the phrase would otherwise describe everything. A government that purchases a commercial model for its own use does not constitute the complex. A provider that answers a lawful subpoena does not constitute it. A company that chooses to build safety filters into its own product does not constitute it, whatever one thinks of the filters; the Hugging Face engineers were inconvenienced by a design choice, not by a regime. A law requiring a frontier laboratory to report a serious security incident does not constitute it either.

None of these is the structure. Each is a part that could belong to it.

The dangerous structure emerges when the cognitive substrate itself becomes an administrative control plane: when the systems through which people think are the same systems through which the state sees, permits, and enforces, and there is nowhere else to think. The previous essay in this series argued that in the domain of association the danger lives not in the parts but in their composition.[22] The same is true here, with one difference. In that domain the composed thing watches what citizens do together. In this one it sits between a citizen and her own reasoning.

## XIV. Permissioned Cognition

The nightmare, if it comes, will not look like the one the culture has rehearsed. Not *Nineteen Eighty-Four*; not officers confiscating graphics cards. Imagine instead a system that is convenient, administratively rational, and, at its inception, well-intentioned.

Advanced models run only on authenticated hardware, because attestation is how the safety case is verified. Powerful inference requires an identity, because accountability requires a name. The hardware attests its compliance continuously, because a one-time check is easily spoofed. Model weights carry approved signatures, because unsigned weights cannot be recalled. Providers update their classifications remotely, because threats change. Certain categories of activity generate mandatory records, because incidents must be investigable after the fact. Sensitive capability requires an authorized account, because capability is what the whole apparatus exists to govern. Circumvention disables acceleration, because a rule with no enforcement is a suggestion. Private AI memory is durable, because users want a system that knows them. And the same memory is searchable, because that is what makes the safety systems work.

None of the individual steps is tyrannical. That is the point.

Each has a reason, most of the reasons are good, and several of them are reasons this essay has itself accepted. The dystopian possibility is emergent: it belongs to the composition, and no one has to intend it for it to arrive. Deleuze is more apt than Orwell here, because what results is not a prohibition pronounced once but a modulation applied continuously, "codes that mark access to information, or reject it."[34] And Shklar supplies the question that must be asked of the composed thing rather than of its parts. Do not ask only whether the present administrators are benevolent. Ask what relation of power the architecture establishes between the weak and the powerful, and what the powerful could do with it if they chose.[26]

The endpoint has a name. Permissioned cognition is the condition in which access to practically important artificial extensions of reasoning exists only through revocable institutional authorization. It is compatible with elections, courts, and a free press. It requires no ministry and no informers. It requires only that the machinery through which people increasingly think be governed at the layer where people meet it, and that the exits be closed.

What would falsify the warning can be said exactly. If, some years from now, the governance of frontier capability has been built at the first and fifth layers, with hosted inference recorded no more than commerce and the named incidents require and local inference untouched, then the architecture described above was a fear and not a forecast, and this essay will have been wrong in the way it would prefer to be wrong. If instead an identity has become a condition of capable inference, unsigned weights a legal hazard, and a person's record of questions a standing object of administrative search, it will have been early. Either outcome is checkable.

## XV. Cognitive Federalism

The alternative is a structure, not a program; a program would be a claim to know more than anyone knows. A society can contain commercial frontier models and locally run ones; several providers rather than one; independent archives, universities, libraries, and journalists; primary documents that a synthesis cannot silently revise; government systems and private systems, open systems and restricted high-risk systems; and different institutional rules at the different boundaries between them. What such a society lacks is a single layer that is sovereign over cognition as such. One could call this cognitive federalism; the deeper concept is older and does not need a new name. It is pluralism.

The traditions the fourth section assembled converge here, and the convergence is stronger than any of them because each supplies what another lacks. Berlin explains why plural values generate a duty to protect meaningful choice: the alternative is an institution that defines a person's "real" interests and proceeds "to bully, oppress, torture in the name, and on behalf, of their 'real' selves."[23] But pluralism about values does not by itself say which liberties come first when administration wants them traded away; Rawls does, placing liberty of conscience among the basic liberties that take priority over the ordinary gains of welfare and convenience.[25] Priority does not explain why an institution that happens to be right may still not be exempted from contradiction; Mill's fallibilism does. Fallibilism does not reach the provider who never interferes; Pettit's non-domination does, and forbids dependence on an uncontestable gatekeeper however benevolent. Non-domination names a status, and Shklar supplies the method for protecting it, which is to design against the foreseeable abuse of concentrated coercive capacity rather than to trust its present holders. Beneath all of them are the two oldest commitments: Spinoza's, that authority stops at inward judgment, and Kant's, that reason is not to be delegated permanently to guardians. None is sufficient alone, and together they do not become one doctrine. They become a structure in which no layer is sovereign over cognition as such.

## The Last Private Domain

In Milton's masque the Lady is bound in the enchanter's chair, and she answers him: "Thou canst not touch the freedom of my mind / With all thy charms, although this corporal rind / Thou hast immanacled."[50] The confidence of the line depended partly on the state of technology. The body could be imprisoned. Books could be burned. Speech could be forbidden and printing licensed. But inward reflection remained extraordinarily difficult to observe, and a right that could not be violated needed no defense.

Modern surveillance weakened that boundary from the outside, by inference from conduct, and the previous essay in this series described how far the inference now reaches. Artificial intelligence does something stranger. People are moving parts of the inner dialogue outward, voluntarily, because doing so makes them more capable, and the technology is powerful precisely because thought no longer has to stay isolated inside the thinker. The paradox follows from the capability and cannot be separated from it. The machinery that enlarges the mind may also make the mind administratively visible.

The conclusion is not that artificial intelligence must remain ungoverned. It is that its governance confronts two forms of loss of control, and that the debate has been conducted as if there were one. The first is the loss of human control over machines. The second is the loss of individual control over the machinery through which humans increasingly think. A liberal civilization has reasons to fear both, and the July incident showed both in the same week.

No artificial model is identical with reason. No company is identical with reason. No state is identical with reason; no safety institution; no dissenting individual. Truth exceeds all of them, which is why the constitution of a society that thinks with machines has to preserve the possibility that each of them can be contradicted, and why that possibility has to be built rather than promised.

Make dangerous power governable without making thought governable. The mind may extend beyond the skull. The freedom of the mind must be allowed to follow it.
