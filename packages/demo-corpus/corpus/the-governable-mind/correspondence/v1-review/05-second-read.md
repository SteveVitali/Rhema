# Independent second read — v1 (fresh context, draft only)

Read cold: `drafts/v1.md` only, all 366 lines, no correspondence, no manifest, no fact-checking. Line numbers below refer to that file. The body runs to roughly 11,500 words before the reference list (13,260 with it), in fifteen numbered sections plus a prologue and coda.

## 1. The thesis

Because large language models now perform outside the skull the functions liberalism protected precisely because they were inside it (questioning, rehearsal, self-criticism, the revision of belief), the governance of AI is not product regulation or speech regulation but, in growing part, regulation of the process by which persons form beliefs, and the record of that process is a new, searchable object of institutional legibility. The constitutional task is therefore to govern genuinely dangerous capability at the layers (frontier training, external agency) whose enforcement never needs to see what a person asked, while preserving epistemic exit (above all locally run, openly distributed models) so that no institution, public or private, becomes sovereign over cognition as such.

## 2. Five most serious objections

### (i) The opening exhibit proves a different thesis than the one it is made to carry (lines 12–16; conceded at line 276)

The strongest version of the passage: the July incident really is double-edged, and the essay's willingness to hand the safety camp its exhibit in the first paragraph, before making its own case, is a mark of seriousness. The second half does show something true and under-told: provider-side restrictions do not travel with the weights, so the intelligence the defenders controlled was the intelligence that served them, and "the exit, when it was needed" was real. But line 16 draws the moral as a claim about *institutions*: the incident "showed ... why it may be dangerous for intelligence to be governable only through institutions." The whole of Section V then argues that corporate refusal and state power are "different species of political problem" (118), and line 276 says outright that "the Hugging Face engineers were inconvenienced by a design choice, not by a regime." So the prologue uses a corporate-shaped fact to do state-shaped work, and the essay later retracts the framing without going back to fix it. What the second half actually demonstrates is Pettit's point (dependence on a gatekeeper who is not answerable) plus the practical value of exit, which is a fine thing to demonstrate, but it should be named as that. And the most interesting sentence in the prologue, "the exit, when it was needed, came from Beijing" (14), is dropped and never recovered: Section VIII treats China as the demonstration of what unconstrained affordances do, and Section XV's pluralism depends on a plural supply of exits, yet nowhere does the essay reckon with the fact that the exit-preserving open model was produced under the regime it holds up as the warning. **Fix:** rewrite line 16 as the non-domination exhibit ("the second half is not about the state; it is about what dependence costs when the guardian is not answerable"), reserve the sovereign-power claim for Section V where it is earned, and give Beijing a paragraph, most naturally at the end of V or in XV, on what it means for cognitive federalism that the market in exits is partly authoritarian in origin.

### (ii) The *externalized forum internum* is established by stipulation, and the disanalogy hidden in the diary is the one that matters (lines 74, 82–88, 154–156, 276, 306)

The strongest version: the functional definition at 82 is a real advance over the neuro-rights literature it cites, because it sidesteps the metaphysics of extended mind (the essay is right, at 58–62, that it does not need Clark and Chalmers) and appeals instead to the older tradition that protected *media*: letters, library records, the sealed ballot. The "developmental history of a belief" (88) is a vivid and probably correct description of what long-lived memory preserves. But the definition's closing clause, "without thereby becoming public conduct," assumes what the argument has to show. A prompt to a hosted service is a communication to a corporation that processes it, may train on it, classifies every line of it for its own safety purposes, and is bound by its own terms rather than by any privilege. The diary analogy at 84 conceals exactly this: no one else wrote in the diary, and no one else is a party to it. The nearer analogies are the letter to a lawyer, the confession to a priest, the query to a librarian, each a relationship in which confidentiality had to be *earned by a doctrine* (privilege, professional duty, library-records statutes) explaining why disclosure to a counterparty did not waive it. The essay proposes no such doctrine for the model provider. It notes at 306 that people externalize "voluntarily" and moves on, which leaves General Comment 22's "no one can be compelled to reveal his thoughts" (74) doing no work at all, since these thoughts were volunteered. The problem compounds in VII: the definition of cognitive legibility at 154 ("the capacity of an institution to reconstruct, classify, or infer aspects of a person's deliberative life from the technical traces") is already fully satisfied by every hosted provider today, by machine, at scale, and line 276 waves this away as "a design choice." The reader cannot tell whether the essay regards privately achieved cognitive legibility as a present harm, a latent one, or none. **Fix:** a section, or a long paragraph at the end of III, that names the third-party problem squarely and argues for a privilege-like or library-records-like status for the cognitive record, with its limits; and one sentence in VII or XIII stating whether legibility achieved privately counts, and why the four-condition definition at 272 requires the state before it counts.

### (iii) Section IX's "probability shaping" is orphaned by the constitutional test and by the falsification criterion (lines 94, 196–198, 202, 250–262, 294)

The strongest version: IX is among the best sections in the piece. The demotion of Newspeak (impossibility, refuted by Fedorenko et al.) in favor of Orwell 1946 (habit, probability) is exactly right, and the move to Arendt at 202 identifies a danger deeper than surveillance: that the intermediary shapes which objection is volunteered, which evidence retrieved, and that the synthesis can change silently while the archive recedes. Line 94 explicitly promises this will be delivered, and it is. But the payoff sections do not know IX exists. Every one of the seven questions at 250–262 is about what the authority needs to *know*, what gateways it creates, and what exits survive; none asks what the intermediary *shapes*, whether the persistent record remains reachable beneath the fluent summary, or whether a change in an answer is discoverable by the person who received the earlier one. The falsification criterion at 294 is stated wholly in terms of identity, signatures, and searchable records. A future that passes it, with local inference untouched and hosted records minimal, could still be the world of 202: continuously re-ranked, silently corrected synthesis, with the disagreeable record no longer reachable. That is the essay's most original worry, and its own test cannot detect it. **Fix:** an eighth question, "archival reachability and disclosure of change" (does the primary record remain reachable beneath the synthesis; is an alteration in output discoverable and contestable?), which also gives the "independent archives ... primary documents that a synthesis cannot silently revise" clause at 298 something to hang on; and a corresponding clause in the falsification paragraph at 294. If the author declines this, then 94 and 196–202 must be cut back to what XII can handle, which would be a loss.

### (iv) *Areopagitica* is loaded in Sections I and VI and never fired at the one live target: model distribution (lines 38, 136, 214, 218, 230, 256, 266–268)

The strongest version: the essay is scrupulous about the hard case. It concedes that weights cannot be recalled, quotes the Safety Report's conditional fairly (214), reports Anthropic's "never advocated for a ban" position accurately (218), and correctly identifies layer 2 as "the hard case" (230). Its own preferred exit, local inference, is at layer 4, and at 268 it even turns the test against its own side. But having said layer 2 is the hard case, it never adjudicates it. Whether there is anything to run locally is decided at layer 2, and the concrete proposal in play there, mandatory pre-release safety testing for "all sufficiently capable models, open and closed" (218), is, structurally, the thing Milton attacked: certification before encounter, administered through a small number of private bodies with the state's blessing. The essay itself writes "*Areopagitica* is the reply to that Ordinance" (136) and "The Stationers were a chokepoint before they were a censor" (256). Then the test at 266 is run on a classified benchmarking process (an easy pass) and on a hypothetical local model given a network interface (an easy fail for the essay's own side), and never on the actual policy question. A senior reader concludes that the author knows the answer and does not want to print it, either because it is "pre-release testing for open weights fails on chokepoint creation and reversibility," which puts the essay at odds with the safety consensus it has just credited, or because it passes on layer fit and externality specificity, which blunts the Milton framing. Either answer is publishable; the silence is not. **Fix:** one paragraph at 266, running the seven questions on mandatory pre-release testing for open-weight release, saying which it passes, which it fails, and what a version that passed would need (a published standard, a right to contest, a sunset, no signature requirement at the point of execution). This is the paragraph the essay exists to write.

### (v) Load-bearing moves are imported from the author's prior essays rather than made (lines 46, 66, 90, 148, 152, 204, 248, 280, 306)

The strongest version: a series may build, every import is flagged, and the cross-references show a system rather than a set of occasional pieces. But nine invocations is a lot, and several *settle* rather than gesture: the chilling injury "does not need re-arguing here" (90); inherited capacity "waits" and this essay "takes the point as settled" (148); "behavioral legibility" (152) is quoted from the author's own coinage as though it were an established term; the "seven criteria, from compute normalization to reopenability" (248) are invisible to this reader, yet the constitutional test is introduced as their sibling; and "the judge ... must remain judgeable" (204) is the clinching line of Section IX's epistemic liberalism and is borrowed. A reader at an ideas publication reads cold. As written, the argument has holes where the reader is sent elsewhere, and the piece cannot stand alone. **Fix:** internalize the three imports that carry weight, in two or three sentences each (chilling as the injury that leaves no defendant; behavioral legibility, defined once, without the self-quotation; the "no finite mind is reason as such / the judge must remain judgeable" idea at 46 and 204), and delete the remaining cross-references outright, or publish with an editor's note that this is the third of three and accept the cost.

## 3. Three strongest things

### (a) The Stationers' Company as the origin of both press freedom and public-private information control (lines 128–138, 256)

The claim that the genealogy of the freedom of inquiry and the genealogy of public-private information control "intersect at their origin" (136) is the essay's best historical move, and it is made honestly: the two cautions at 132 (Deazley and Clegg on the ad hoc character of Tudor censorship; the Licensing Act's intermittency) make the mechanism more credible rather than less, because the general proposition at 134 (concentration produces legibility; neither party need intend the other's ends) survives them. "Milton was not arguing against the state alone. He was arguing against a state that had found, in a guild, an instrument" (138) and "The Stationers were a chokepoint before they were a censor" (256) are the sentences this piece should be known for.

### (b) The five layers and the "minimum necessary legibility" question (lines 226–244, 254)

Turning "AI regulation" into five objects with different informational requirements is the essay's most usable analytical contribution, and the question at 254, "whether compliance can be established while revealing less than the rule's drafters first asked for," is a diagnostic that a policy reader could adopt tomorrow. "The mechanisms differ in what they need to know" (242) is the one-line paragraph in the piece that fully earns its isolation. The section also has the right shape of modesty: 244 says the surveillance-shaped rules are not inevitable, only available and easiest.

### (c) "A synthesis is not a record" (line 202)

"A book published in 1972 says tomorrow what it says today. A model's answer about 1972 can change, and change silently." This is a genuinely new statement of a problem, located precisely in Arendt's own premise (that opinion presupposes facts stubborn enough to be disagreed about), and it converts her "factual world" from a given into a design requirement. It is the most original idea in the essay, which is why objection (iii) matters. Honorable mention: the falsification paragraph at 294. An essay of this kind almost never says what would prove it wrong; this one does, checkably, and that should be preserved through every revision.

## 4. Verdict: major revisions

This is a serious, well-read, and in places original piece, with three contributions (the Stationers, the layers, the synthesis/record distinction) that would survive any amount of cutting, and a level of sourcing rare in the genre. It is not yet a publishable essay for a cold reader, for reasons that are structural rather than cosmetic: the prologue's moral is retracted at 276 without repair; the central concept is stipulated past the third-party problem that a lawyer or a skeptical reader raises in the first minute; the most original section (IX) is disconnected from the test and the falsification that are supposed to be the payoff; the one policy question the whole Milton apparatus is built to answer (pre-release certification of open weights) is set up and then avoided; and nine load-bearing moves are outsourced to essays the reader has not seen. None of these requires new research; all require the author to say things the draft currently declines to say. The piece is also about a third too long for its argument: Sections IV and XV overlap substantially (the roll-call at 300 is a syllabus recap of 100–108), and the stylistic devices noted below are inflating the count. A revision that fixed (i)–(v), compressed IV/XV, and came in near 8,000 words would be a strong acceptance.

## Smaller notes (line-level, not counted among the five)

- **The one-sentence-paragraph device** is used as an aphoristic beat at 58, 62, 68, 70, 92, 122, 138, 144, 146, 162, 176, 178, 212, 242, 264, 278, 288. It works the first three or four times; by Section VI the reader anticipates the drop and it reads as a mannerism. Keep 68–70, 122, 178, 212, 242, 264; fold the rest back into their paragraphs.
- **Self-announced modesty** is a tic: "kept small here" (100), "defined narrowly enough to be believed" (152), "and nothing more" (160), "The honest caveat belongs beside the fact" (186), "is narrow" (188, 204), "The precision matters" (276), "and it is not the claim this essay makes" (118). The narrowness should be shown, not announced; cut all of these and the argument reads as more confident, not less.
- Line 42, "The contemporary inversion is exact. ... It follows that": the inversion is not exact (Kant feared delegation of judgment; the machine invites collaboration in it), and "it follows" covers an inference that is asserted. Say "nearly" and make the inference.
- Line 160, "Foucault is the most cited and most misread authority": what follows is the standard reading. Cut the boast.
- Lines 100–106: Pettit and Rawls are quoted from the Stanford Encyclopedia rather than from Pettit and Rawls. At this level, "citizens' equal liberty must have priority over economic policy" should be Rawls's sentence or be paraphrased without quotation marks.
- Line 192: note [40] is cited before [39]; the numbering is out of order.
- Lines 172 and 188 both say the authoritarian cases are not predictions; once is enough.
