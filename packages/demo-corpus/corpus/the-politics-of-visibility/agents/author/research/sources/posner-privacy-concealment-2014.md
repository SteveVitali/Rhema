# posner-privacy-concealment-2014
- **URL:** https://reason.com/2014/12/08/richard-posner-privacy-is-mainly-about-c/ · **Accessed:** 2026-08-25 · **For:** v1→v2 response round, answering Posner's collection/use counterargument with accurate quotation
- **What it establishes:** The security-side position at its most candid, from its most distinguished proponent. (1) Reason (Nick Gillespie, Dec. 8, 2014), relaying PC World's report of Judge Richard Posner's remarks at a Georgetown Law cybercrime/privacy conference (Dec. 2014) — all quotes below verified against the Reason page. Posner: privacy is largely concealment of discreditable conduct; NSA bulk collection is unobjectionable; privacy should carry almost no weight against national security; he was "shocked" that a manufacturer could build a phone the government cannot search; Congress should give the NSA "carte blanche." (2) The cleanest statement of the collection/use distinction in the security literature is Posner's own earlier op-ed, Richard A. Posner, *Our Domestic Intelligence Crisis*, Wash. Post, Dec. 21, 2005 — VERIFIED VERBATIM via Daniel Solove, *"I've Got Nothing to Hide" and Other Misunderstandings of Privacy*, 44 San Diego L. Rev. 745 (2007), which quotes it at length: machine collection and computer sifting of data "cannot, as such, invade privacy" because "a computer is not a sentient being"; any privacy question arises only at the point of human use. This is the argument the essay must answer: collection is harmless because no human sees the data, and harm (if any) occurs at use, where it can in principle be regulated and audited. Related Posner statements of the concealment thesis with book/page cites (verified as quoted by Solove): "[W]hen people today decry lack of privacy, what they want, I think, is mainly … more power to conceal information about themselves that others might use to their disadvantage" (Richard A. Posner, The Economics of Justice 271 (1983 ed.)); privacy as the "right to conceal discreditable facts about himself" (Posner, Economic Analysis of Law). Also on the trail but NOT verified here: Posner, *Privacy, Surveillance, and Law*, 75 U. Chi. L. Rev. 245 (2008) (search-level; verify before print quotation) — reportedly elaborates the same machine-collection/use-audit position; and Stewart Baker (e.g., Skating on Stilts, 2010) as the policy-community version of the same argument (search-level; verify before print quotation).
- **Key passages:** "Much of what passes for the name of privacy is really just trying to conceal the disreputable parts of your conduct" (Posner, per Reason/PC World, Dec. 2014; verified against Reason page). "Privacy is mainly about trying to improve your social and business opportunities by concealing the sorts of bad activities that would cause other people not to want to deal with you." (id., verified). "If the NSA wants to vacuum all the trillions of bits of information that are crawling through the electronic worldwide networks, I think that's fine" (id., verified). "Privacy interests should really have very little weight when you're talking about national security" (id., verified). "I'm shocked at the thought that a company would be permitted to manufacture an electronic product that the government would not be able to search" (id., verified). Collection/use distinction, Posner 2005 (verified via Solove 2007 quoting the op-ed): "The collection, mainly through electronic means, of vast amounts of personal data is said to invade privacy. But machine collection and processing of data cannot, as such, invade privacy. Because of their volume, the data are first sifted by computers, which search for names, addresses, phone numbers, etc., that may have intelligence value. This initial sifting, far from invading privacy (a computer is not a sentient being), keeps most private data from being read by any intelligence officer." [Note: Solove's PDF OCR renders one instance as "shifting"; the op-ed's word is "sifting" — confirm against the Washington Post original before print.]
- **Reliability:** Reason piece: secondary (libertarian outlet quoting PC World's conference report), but the quotes are consistently reported across outlets and Reason is ideologically *hostile* to Posner's position, so no incentive to sharpen his words in his favor. Posner 2005 quote: primary text verified through a peer-reviewed law-review article (Solove) quoting it in full — strong. Posner is a primary and maximally authoritative source for the security-side position (sitting federal appellate judge, leading law-and-economics scholar), which is precisely why he is the right counterargument to engage.
- **Relevance to this project:** Supplies the essay's steel-manned opposition for the collection/use section: (a) the concealment thesis (privacy = hiding discreditable conduct), and (b) the collection/use distinction (machine collection is not harm; harm occurs at human use, which can be audited). The 2014 conference quotes are for accurate attribution of the blunt position; the 2005 op-ed passage is the analytically serious version the argument section must actually defeat (e.g., via chilling effects operating at the point of *collection awareness*, per Penney/Stoycheff — behavior changes whether or not any human ever reads the data, so the harm precedes "use").
