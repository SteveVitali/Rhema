# richards-dangers-of-surveillance-2013

- **URL:** https://harvardlawreview.org/wp-content/uploads/2013/05/vol126_richards.pdf (verified from full PDF via Wayback mirror of the same file; publisher URL currently 403s to bots) · **Accessed:** 2026-08-25 · **For:** v1→v2 response round, accurate citation of the intellectual-privacy and power-asymmetry predecessors
- **What it establishes:** Richards's two-part account of why surveillance harms: (1) intellectual surveillance chills experimentation with ideas (the "intellectual privacy" argument); (2) surveillance shifts the power dynamic between watcher and watched, enabling blackmail, discrimination, coercion, selective enforcement, and persuasion. Also his four normative principles (surveillance transcends the public/private divide; secret surveillance is illegitimate; total surveillance is illegitimate; surveillance is a cognizable harm). Chilling-effect and power-asymmetry arguments in the essay must be credited here (and, for intellectual privacy, to his 2008 Texas L. Rev. article).
- **Key passages:** (verified verbatim against the PDF, 126 Harv. L. Rev. 1934 (2013); hyphenation/spacing artifacts of PDF extraction normalized)
  - Intellectual privacy (p. 1935): "First, surveillance is harmful because it can chill the exercise of our civil liberties. With respect to civil liberties, consider surveillance of people when they are thinking, reading, and communicating with others in order to make up their minds about political and social issues. Such intellectual surveillance is especially dangerous because it can cause people not to experiment with new, controversial, or deviant ideas. To protect our intellectual freedom to think without state oversight or interference, we need what I have elsewhere called 'intellectual privacy.'"
  - Power asymmetry (pp. 1935–36): "A second special harm that surveillance poses is its effect on the power dynamic between the watcher and the watched. This disparity creates the risk of a variety of harms, such as discrimination, coercion, and the threat of selective enforcement, where critics of the government can be prosecuted or blackmailed for wrongdoing unrelated to the purpose of the surveillance."
  - Elaboration (p. 1953): "Critically, the gathering of information affects the power dynamic between the watcher and the watched, giving the watcher greater power to influence or direct the subject of surveillance. … The power effects of surveillance illustrate three additional dangers of surveillance: blackmail, discrimination, and persuasion." (Followed by the FBI/Martin Luther King, Jr. wiretap example.)
  - Two-line thesis (Part I close): "first, that surveillance by government and private actors threatens intellectual privacy and chills the exercise of vital civil liberties; and second, that surveillance affects the power balance between individuals and those who are watching, increasing the risk of persuasion, blackmail, and other harmful uses of sensitive information by others."
- **Reliability:** Primary source (the article itself); Harvard Law Review, peer-edited; Richards (Washington University) is a principal figure in surveillance-law scholarship. Independent.
- **Relevance to this project:** Bears on any chilling-effects claim, any watcher/watched power-asymmetry claim, and the MLK/selective-enforcement class of examples. Note "discrimination, coercion, and the threat of selective enforcement" is the exact triad at pp. 1935–36; the Part IV triad is "blackmail, discrimination, and persuasion" — do not conflate the two lists when quoting.
