- **What it establishes:** Verification status upgraded for three of four. (a) Elizabeth Stoycheff, *Under Surveillance: Examining Facebook's Spiral of Silence Effects in the Wake of NSA Internet Monitoring*, 93 Journalism & Mass Comm. Q. 296–311 (2016) — FULL TEXT REACHED via Sage's free-access token link (journals.sagepub.com/stoken/rbtfl/1jxrYu4cQPtA6/full), read via automated extraction. Design: 255 U.S. adults (Survey Sampling International panel, Jan. 2015); half (n=121) received a surveillance prime stating that the NSA "does monitor the online activities of individual citizens"; all then saw a fictional Facebook post about U.S. airstrikes against ISIS and rated willingness to comment/share/like/post. Findings: the *direct* effect of the surveillance prime on willingness to speak was NOT statistically significant; the chilling effect appeared as an interaction — primed participants who also believed surveillance is *justified/necessary* were significantly less willing to express minority views (heightened spiral-of-silence conformity), while those who saw surveillance as unjustified were largely unaffected. This nuance matters: the study does not show blanket silencing. (b) Elizabeth Stoycheff, Juan Liu, Kai Xu & Kunto Wibowo, *Privacy and the Panopticon: Online Mass Surveillance's Deterrence and Chilling Effects*, 21 New Media & Society 602–619 (2019; first published online 2018) — ABSTRACT LEVEL ONLY (Sage 403-blocked; abstract via search and repository records). Abstract-level findings: Study 1 — perceived surveillance "significantly deters individuals' intentions to engage in illegal offenses, an effect that extends to political, but not privacy-protective behaviors"; Study 2 — replication with a sample of U.S.-resident Muslims, indicating the chilling effects "are not specific to any one online population, experimental stimuli, or political context." (search-level; verify before print quotation). (c) Gaétan Nandong, *State surveillance and collective dissent*, Am. J. Pol. Sci. (2026), doi 10.1111/ajps.70057, Early View (published July 1, 2026), pp. 1–15 — ABSTRACT VERIFIED VERBATIM from Wiley. Important scoping fact: this is a FORMAL MODEL (game-theoretic, incomplete information), not behavioral data — it cannot serve as empirical chilling-effects evidence, and it cuts both ways: surveillance can create "a climate of fear" but "can also stimulate dissent" and enable coordination, with "lower expected levels of repression." (d) Aaron Rosenthal & Saher Selod, *Racialized Surveillance and Voting: Connecting Government Monitoring to American Muslim Electoral Participation*, Genealogy 10(2):50 (2026, published Apr. 24, 2026) — FULL TEXT READ (via text-mode retrieval; mdpi.com direct fetch 403s but content obtained in full). Data: Pew Research Center's 2017 nationally representative survey of 1,000 American Muslims; participation measures (citizens only, ~80% of sample): registration (74%) and 2016 presidential turnout (56%). Surveillance-encounter measures: singled out as Muslim by law enforcement in the past year (7.2%) vs. by airport security/TSA (19.2%). THE ACTUAL FINDING, precisely scoped: being singled out by the POLICE is associated with significantly lower registration and turnout (a "quite potent" disengaging effect); being singled out by AIRPORT SECURITY shows NULL effects on both. Attitudes split too: WORRY about government surveillance is associated with HIGHER turnout (mobilization), while believing surveillance is LIKELY/inevitable is associated with LOWER turnout. Racial variation: Black Muslims most likely to encounter police surveillance (16.2%, over 20% among U.S.-born); Asian Muslims report the most airport monitoring. Limitations (authors'): cross-sectional observational survey data; only two electoral outcomes; cannot probe mechanisms.
- **Key passages:** (a) Stoycheff 2016: "knowing one's online activities are subject to government interception and believing these surveillance practices are necessary for national security play important roles in influencing conformist behavior" (full text reached via automated extraction; spot-verify wording against the Sage page before print quotation). In contrast, experiencing discriminatory surveillance in the airport has no impact on electoral engagement." And: "we find that police surveillance dampens electoral participation, but no similar impact is found from increased monitoring in airports."
