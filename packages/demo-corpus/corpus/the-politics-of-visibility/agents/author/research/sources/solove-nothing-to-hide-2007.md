# solove-nothing-to-hide-2007

- **URL:** https://digital.sandiego.edu/sdlr/vol44/iss4/5/ (PDF: https://digital.sandiego.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=2790&context=sdlr; verified from full PDF via Wayback mirror of the same file) · **Accessed:** 2026-08-25 · **For:** v1→v2 response round, accurate citation of the canonical "nothing to hide" rebuttal
- **What it establishes:** Solove's canonical dismantling of the "nothing to hide" argument: it presupposes a narrow, disclosure-of-bad-secrets conception of privacy; the real harms of government data collection are aggregation, exclusion, secondary use — Kafkaesque bureaucratic harms of powerlessness, not (only) Orwellian inhibition; privacy harms rarely produce visible casualties, which is why they are politically undervalued. Any "nothing to hide" discussion in the essay is downstream of this piece.
- **Key passages:** (verified verbatim against the PDF, 44 San Diego L. Rev. 745 (2007))
  - Aggregation (pp. 766–67): "One such harm, for example, which I call aggregation, emerges from the combination of small bits of seemingly innocuous data. When combined, the information becomes much more telling about a person. … Aggregation, however, means that by combining pieces of information we might not care to conceal, the government can glean information about us that we might really want to conceal."
  - Kafka framing (same passage, pp. 766–67): "the problems are not just Orwellian, but Kafkaesque. The NSA programs are problematic even if no information people want to hide is uncovered. In The Trial, the problem is not inhibited behavior, but rather a suffocating powerlessness and vulnerability created by the court system's use of personal data and its exclusion of the protagonist from having any knowledge or participation in the process."
  - Dead bodies (p. 768): "Most privacy problems lack dead bodies. Of course, there are exceptional cases such as the murders of Rebecca Shaeffer and Amy Boyer. … These examples aside, there is not a lot of death and gore in privacy law. If this is the standard to recognize a problem, then few privacy problems will be recognized." (Exact sentence is "Most privacy problems lack dead bodies." — responding to Ann Bartow's complaint that his taxonomy lacks "enough dead bodies.")
  - Closing (p. 772): "When engaged with directly, the nothing to hide argument can ensnare, for it forces the debate to focus on its narrow understanding of privacy. But when confronted with the plurality of privacy problems implicated by government data collection and use beyond surveillance and disclosure, the nothing to hide argument, in the end, has nothing to say."
- **Reliability:** Primary source (the symposium article itself, via the University of San Diego's official repository); Solove is the leading privacy-law taxonomist (GW Law). Independent.
