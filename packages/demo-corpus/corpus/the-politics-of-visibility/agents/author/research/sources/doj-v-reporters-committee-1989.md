# doj-v-reporters-committee-1989
- **URL:** https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep489/usrep489749/usrep489749.pdf (Library of Congress scan of U.S. Reports vol. 489; Justia mirror at https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/489/749/ was 403-blocked) · **Accessed:** 2026-08-25 · **For:** v1→v2 response round, "practical obscurity" anchor — compilation changes the privacy calculus
- **What it establishes:** *U.S. Dept. of Justice v. Reporters Committee for Freedom of the Press*, 489 U.S. 749 (1989) (Stevens, J., for a unanimous Court in judgment; 7-Justice majority opinion) held that disclosure of FBI rap sheets to third parties "could reasonably be expected to constitute an unwarranted invasion of personal privacy" under FOIA Exemption 7(C), categorically. The Court recognized a distinct privacy interest in the *practical obscurity* of information that is individually public but scattered — compiling it into one queryable record creates a new privacy harm. This is the Supreme Court's own aggregation principle, pre-digital.
- **Key passages:** (verbatim from U.S. Reports text)
  - The compilation difference (489 U.S. at 764): "Granted, in many contexts the fact that information is not freely available is no reason to exempt that information from a statute generally requiring its dissemination. But the issue here is whether the compilation of otherwise hard-to-obtain information alters the privacy interest implicated by disclosure of that information. Plainly there is a vast difference between the public records that might be found after a diligent search of courthouse files, county archives, and local police stations throughout the country and a computerized summary located in a single clearinghouse of information."
  - The "practical obscurity" holding (489 U.S. at 780): "the privacy interest in maintaining the practical obscurity of rap-sheet information will always be high. When the subject of such a rap sheet is a private citizen and when the information is in the Government's control as a compilation, rather than as a record of 'what the Government is up to,' the privacy interest protected by Exemption 7(C) is in fact at its apex while the FOIA-based public interest in disclosure is at its nadir."
  - Syllabus phrasing (489 U.S. at 749–50, headnote — not citable as the Court's words): "the privacy interest in maintaining the rap sheet's 'practical obscurity' is always at its apex while the FOIA-based public interest in disclosure is at its nadir."
- **Reliability:** Primary (Library of Congress scan of the official U.S. Reports; quotes checked in context of the opinion text). Note it is a FOIA/statutory case, not a constitutional holding — frame it as the Court recognizing the aggregation-privacy principle, not as Fourth Amendment doctrine. Maynard itself analogizes to "a rap sheet," i.e., to this case.
- **Relevance to this project:** Supplies the canonical pre-internet statement that obscurity-by-friction is a real privacy interest and that compilation destroys it — direct doctrinal support for the essay's claim that searchable aggregation is a different act from scattered public visibility. Links forward to Maynard (which borrows the whole-vs-parts logic) and to the essay's "friction as protection" theme (pairs with brandeis-myers-dissent-1926). Cite as: U.S. Dept. of Justice v. Reporters Comm. for Freedom of the Press, 489 U.S. 749, 764, 780 (1989).
