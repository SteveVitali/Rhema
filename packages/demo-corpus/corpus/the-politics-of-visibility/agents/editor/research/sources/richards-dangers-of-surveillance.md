# Richards, "The Dangers of Surveillance" (2013)

- **URL:** https://harvardlawreview.org/wp-content/uploads/2013/05/vol126_richards.pdf (PDF downloaded and read in full)
- **Accessed:** 2026-08-21
- **Cite:** Neil M. Richards, 126 Harv. L. Rev. 1934 (2013).

**Establishes:** The canonical statement of why surveillance harms democracy absent arrest. Two harms: (1) "intellectual surveillance is especially dangerous because it can cause people not to experiment with new, controversial, or deviant ideas" (chilling of "intellectual privacy"); (2) power asymmetry between watcher and watched enabling "discrimination, coercion, and the threat of selective enforcement... blackmail." Notes courts treat "mere surveillance" as creating no harm (Clapper standing problem — parallels the draft's "the constitutional order's alarms are wired to state action"). Four principles: surveillance transcends the public/private divide; secret surveillance is illegitimate; total surveillance is illegitimate; surveillance is a harm for standing purposes. Draft's SX/SXIII runs close; Richards's center is thinking/reading, the draft's is association — a real but citable difference.
