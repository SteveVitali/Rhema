# The last phone booth

There is one phone booth left in my neighborhood, and I have started walking
out of my way to look at it. The handset is gone. Someone has taped a QR code
for a taxi service inside the door, which is the closest thing the booth has
to a user. The city painted over the glass twice last year; both times the
paint peeled within a month, and the second crew did not come back.

The booth was installed in 1988, part of a municipal contract that once put a
payphone on most corners within a mile of the station. In 2001 the carrier
reported more than two thousand booths in service across the county. By 2019
the count was under sixty, and the contract quietly expired. What remains is
not a service. It is furniture the network forgot to remove.

We tend to treat infrastructure as permanent until the day it vanishes. The
booth is useful to me now precisely because it is useless — a small monument
to the assumption that a shared, anonymous way to reach anyone was a public
good worth maintaining on a corner. Nobody decided to keep one booth standing
as a memorial. The removal schedule just ended, and this one was next.

My neighborhood now expects every resident to carry a charged private
telephone. The elderly neighbor who still writes numbers on paper scraps is
the only person I know who used the booth within its final decade, and she
used it because it worked when her electricity did not. When I pass it I think
about which of today's conveniences will end the same way — unretired,
unannounced, just no longer answered.

The paint will peel again. At some point a crew will finally take the shell
down, and the corner will look like it always did. The memory of a public
phone will belong to the people who happened to notice the last one.
