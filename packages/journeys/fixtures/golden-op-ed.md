# The city council voted on Tuesday. Its broadband maps are still wrong.

On Tuesday the council approved another round of broadband subsidies, and
congratulated itself in the usual way. The vote relied on coverage maps the
state published two years ago — maps that mark whole census blocks "served"
when a single house on one corner can get a connection.

I spent the spring talking to households those maps call connected. On Ridge
Road the fiber line stops four poles short of the last mailbox. The family at
the end of it runs a propane business over a phone hotspot, and their kid does
homework in the school parking lot because the library closes at five.

This is not a mystery of technology. It is a bookkeeping choice. The subsidy
program pays providers to report coverage, not to prove it, and nobody in the
capital has an incentive to knock on doors and check. Every block the map
overstates is a block the next round of money will skip, because on paper the
problem is already solved.

The fix is boring and cheap. Require providers to file address-level service
claims before they are paid, publish the raw filings, and let residents
challenge a "served" flag with a photograph of their dead jack. Other states
already do this; the first one to implement it found a third of its "covered"
blocks weren't.

Tuesday's vote spends real money against a fictional map. Before the council
writes the next check, it should demand addresses instead of acreage. The
people on Ridge Road can wait for fiber. They should not have to wait for
their government to notice the map is wrong.
