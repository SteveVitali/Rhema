## A short history of the free library

The idea that a city should lend books to anyone, for nothing, is younger than it seems. The Boston
Public Library, established in 1848, is usually described as the first large free municipal library
in the United States, and its founding argument was explicitly democratic: an educated public was
the precondition of self-government. The Library of Congress, founded in 1850, served legislators
rather than citizens for most of its first century.

The great expansion came with Andrew Carnegie, whose grants paid for well over a thousand library
buildings across the United States between the 1880s and 1919. Carnegie's condition was that the
town supply the land and commit to funding operations every year — a detail that matters, because
it made the library a standing line in the municipal budget rather than a gift that could be allowed
to lapse. The New York Public Library's main building on Fifth Avenue opened to the public in 1911,
and it was designed as a reading room for everyone, not a stack for scholars.

What none of these institutions assumed was that reading happened only in daylight.
Nineteenth-century reading rooms routinely stayed open into the evening, precisely because their
patrons worked during the day.
